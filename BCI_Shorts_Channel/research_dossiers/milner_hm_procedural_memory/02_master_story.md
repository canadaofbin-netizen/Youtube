# Brenda Milner & Patient H.M. (1962/1968): Tracing a Mirror Star With Zero Memory of Ever Seeing the Pencil — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

On Day 3 of an experiment in 1962, neuropsychologist **Brenda Milner** set a wooden mirror-box contraption down in front of 36-year-old **Henry Molaison** — known to every textbook on Earth as **Patient H.M.** — and asked if he remembered trying it over the past two days. Henry looked at the box politely and said the honest truth: *'No, I have never seen that before in my life.'* Then he picked up the pencil, looked into the backwards mirror, zipped around all five tricky points of a double-bordered star with **almost zero mistakes**, and blinked in surprise: ***'Well, this is strange — I thought this would be difficult, it looks so easy!'*** (SRC1). In that single magical minute, centuries of believing 'memory is one thing' shattered forever.

Recorded in 1962 / 1968, Hartford & Montreal Neurological Institute testing table, the defining visual record centers on a 1960s neuropsychology desk with a mirror-star tracing apparatus (hand shield, vertical mirror, and five-pointed star sheet) where a seated man traces smoothly beside a 3-day error drop chart (SRC1, SRC_STILL). By testing Henry Molaison ('Patient H.M.') across three days on a mirror-reversed star-tracing box at Montreal/Hartford (1962/1968), Brenda Milner proved the human brain runs separate memory engines: the hippocampus records conscious facts and events ('Knowing What'), while the cerebellum and basal ganglia master physical skills ('Knowing How') even when conscious memory is zero (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

At Montreal Neurological Institute (McGill University) & Hartford Hospital, Connecticut in 1968, **Brenda Milner & William Beecher Scoville's Studies of Henry Molaison ('Patient H.M.') & the 1962 3-Day Mirror-Star Tracing Discovery** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Brenda Milner, Suzanne Corkin, H.-L. Teuber (1968). Further analysis of the hippocampal amnesic syndrome: 14-year follow-up study of H.M. Neuropsychologia).

## 3. People

- **Brenda Milner**: Montreal Neurological Institute neuropsychologist (still sharp past age 105!) who traveled down to Connecticut with a mirror-star apparatus and discovered the human brain has MORE THAN ONE memory system (SRC1).
- **Henry Gustav Molaison ('Patient H.M.', 1926–2008)**: 27-year-old Connecticut man who underwent experimental bilateral medial temporal lobe surgery on August 25, 1953 to stop intractable epilepsy — losing new conscious episodic memory while teaching science how memory works (SRC1).
- **Suzanne Corkin & Larry R. Squire**: MIT neuroscientist who worked with Henry for 46 years (and revealed his full name with dignity upon his passing in 2008) and UCSD memory taxonomist who mapped Declarative vs Non-Declarative brain systems (SRC1).

Henry Gustav Molaison (1926-2008) underwent experimental bilateral medial-temporal surgery in Hartford in 1953 for severe epilepsy; McGill's Brenda Milner (b. 1918) and MIT's Suzanne Corkin (1937-2016) partnered respectfully with Henry across 55 years, transforming him into the most important collaborator in neuroscience history.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Milner (1962/1968, SRC1) tested H.M. across three consecutive days (10 trials/day) tracing between the narrow double lines of a five-pointed star seen ONLY in a vertical mirror behind a hand shield, followed by Corkin's (1968, SRC3) rotary-pursuit and bimanual tracking batteries and 1997 high-resolution MRI (SRC2).

On Day 3 he swears politely he has never seen the mirror box in his life — then traces a backwards five-pointed star like an expert on the very first stroke (SRC1, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Despite **0% conscious recall** of ever seeing the mirror apparatus, Henry's mirror-tracing errors plunged steeply across Day 1, carried over overnight to Day 2, and reached sleek, near-errorless mastery on Day 3 — replicated across rotary and bimanual motor skills (SRC1, SRC3).

- On August 25, 1953 at Hartford Hospital, neurosurgeon William Scoville suctioned ~5–8 cm of **bilateral medial temporal tissue** (hippocampus, amygdala, entorhinal cortex) from 27-year-old **Henry Molaison ('H.M.')** to relieve devastating seizures: seizures dropped, Henry's **IQ actually rose slightly (to 118!)**, his humor and manners stayed kind, and he remembered his 1930s-40s childhood — yet **he could never form a single new conscious fact or event memory again**. (SRC1)
- Step out of the testing room for fifteen minutes and walk back in: even after working with **Brenda Milner** and **Suzanne Corkin** for *forty years*, Henry greeted them politely as brand-new strangers. (SRC1)
- Before 1962, virtually every scientist on Earth assumed 'memory' was one single faculty — if H.M. couldn't remember eating lunch 30 minutes ago, surely he could NEVER learn a brand-new motor skill across three days. (SRC1)
- Brenda Milner put that dogma to the test with a brilliant wooden tabletop apparatus: **Mirror-Star Tracing** — a metal shield blocked direct sight of Henry's hand and a printed **five-pointed double-outline star**, forcing him to trace between the narrow 6-mm lines while looking **ONLY into a vertical mirror** (which reverses up/down movements backwards!). (SRC1)
- On **Day 1 (10 trials)**, just like any healthy beginner, Henry's pencil jagged across the borders dozens of times (~30 errors on Trial 1, improving across the afternoon). On **Day 2**, Milner set the apparatus on the table and asked if he remembered it: Henry smiled politely and said **'No, I've never seen this before in my life!'** — then picked up the pencil and started right where Day 1's improved curve left off. (SRC1)
- By **Day 3 (Trial 30)**, Henry zipped smoothly around all five points of the mirror-reversed star with **almost ZERO border errors (<1–2 errors)** — looked down at his flawless star in genuine surprise, and remarked: ***'Well, this is strange — I thought this would be difficult, it looks so easy!'***. (SRC1)
- Suzanne Corkin (1968, *Neuropsychologia*, SRC1) proved Day-3 mirror tracing wasn't a fluke: Henry showed robust day-to-day motor skill learning on **rotary pursuit** and **bimanual tracking** while remaining 0% aware of ever practicing. (SRC1)
- Modern high-resolution MRI in 1997 (Corkin et al., SRC2) and post-mortem histology in 2009-2014 revealed the exact division of labor: **medial temporal / hippocampal circuits** bind conscious **Declarative ('Knowing WHAT')** memories, whereas **basal ganglia, motor cortex, and cerebellum** learn **Procedural ('Knowing HOW')** motor skills completely independently. (SRC2)
- In Milner's bedside working-memory test, Henry Molaison held the three-digit number 584 in mind for 15 minutes through continuous verbal rehearsal, yet forgot both 584 and the test itself the moment his attention was diverted. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. On Day 3, Henry Molaison looks at Brenda Milner's mirror-star contraption, swears honestly **'I have never seen that thing in my life'**, traces the backwards mirror star like a seasoned pro with **near-zero errors**, and says surprised: ***'Huh — I thought that was going to be hard!'*** — a striking result given that one sentence on Day 3 split 'Knowing WHAT' from 'Knowing HOW' forever (SRC1). After 1953, NO neurosurgeon on Earth ever again removed both hippocampi from an epilepsy patient — Henry's sacrifice immediately saved thousands of future patients' memories through unilateral Wada memory pre-testing (invented by Milner & Wada) — notable because one tragic surgical outcome rewrote neurosurgical safety rules worldwide (SRC1). Henry kept a sharp 118 IQ, solved tough crossword puzzles using pre-1953 vocabulary, and could hold a 3-digit number in mind for 15 minutes solely by repeating it non-stop under his breath — until a knock at the door distracted him for two seconds and the number vanished into thin air — revealing how short-term prefrontal rehearsal works 100% independently of hippocampal long-term storage (SRC1). When Henry Molaison passed away peacefully at age 82 in 2008, the entire neuroscience world learned the real name and warm smile behind the two most famous initials in brain history: **H.M.** — highlighting that forty-six years of gentle partnership with Milner and Corkin (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Henry's case delivered THREE simultaneous revolutions: (1) neurosurgeons immediately banned bilateral hippocampal removal forever, saving thousands of future epilepsy patients; (2) because Henry STILL remembered his pre-1950 childhood, the **hippocampus** isn't the permanent warehouse of lifelong memories — it is the temporary binding teacher that transfers episodes into neocortex; and (3) **Procedural ('Knowing How')** motor skills live in the **cerebellum and basal ganglia**, completely independent of conscious **Declarative ('Knowing What')** memory (SRC1, SRC2).

- **"Patient H.M. lost ALL memory — childhood, language, intelligence, and motor learning."** — Henry kept his pre-surgery childhood memories, fluent English, 118 IQ, 20-second working memory, AND full procedural motor skill learning — ONLY consolidating new conscious declarative memories broke. (SRC1)
- **"The hippocampus is the permanent warehouse where your lifelong childhood memories sit forever."** — Because Henry STILL remembered pre-1950 events after hippocampal removal, Milner proved the hippocampus is the **temporary binding teacher** that consolidates memories gradually out into neocortex over years. (SRC1)
- **"Practicing a sport, surgical knot, or musical scale only counts if you consciously analyze every try."** — Basal ganglia and cerebellar motor circuits log error-correction curves across sleep and days even when conscious episodic recall is completely zero. (SRC2)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Every modern textbook diagram splitting Declarative (episodic + semantic) from Non-Declarative (procedural, priming, conditioning) memory traces directly back to Brenda Milner's wooden mirror-star box and Henry Molaison's generous life.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC3** (Suzanne Corkin (1968). Acquisition of motor skill after bilateral medial temporal-lobe excision. Neuropsychologia) extends Brenda Milner's mirror-drawing dissociation in patient H.M. across rotary pursuit, bimanual tracking, and tactile maze learning. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across T. W. Cook (1933). Studies in cross education. I. Mirror tracing the star-shaped maze. Journal of Experimental Psychology (**SRC2**); Robert Zatorre (2018). Brenda Milner and the origins of cognitive neuroscience. Current Biology (**SRC4**); T. W. Cook (1933). Studies in cross education: II. Further experiments in mirror tracing the star-shaped maze. Journal of Experimental Psychology (**SRC5**); and Bryan Kolb (2022). Brenda Milner: Pioneer of the Study of the Human Frontal Lobes. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience (**SRC6**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **hippocampus** (1953 bilateral medial-temporal surgical loss) contributes by blocking consolidation of any new conscious episode so on Day 2 and Day 3 Henry genuinely has zero memory of ever seeing the mirror-star box, which captures Scoville & Milner's foundational declarative amnesia discovery; meanwhile, the **cerebellum** (intact visuomotor error-correction learning) contributes by recalibrating reversed mirror-hand trajectories trial after trial across three consecutive days, which drives the steep 30-to-1 error drop on the mirror star; meanwhile, the **basal ganglia** (permanent procedural skill consolidation) contributes by locking in effortless Day-3 star-tracing mastery alongside motor cortex while prefrontal IQ and manners stay intact, which completes the Declarative vs Procedural double dissociation.

## 8. What it means for you

By testing Henry Molaison ('Patient H.M.') across three days on a mirror-reversed star-tracing box at Montreal/Hartford (1962/1968), Brenda Milner proved the human brain runs separate memory engines: the hippocampus records conscious facts and events ('Knowing What'), while the cerebellum and basal ganglia master physical skills ('Knowing How') even when conscious memory is zero. Trust 'sleep-on-it' physical practice when learning an instrument, sport, typing layout, or surgical skill — your cerebellum and basal ganglia refine motor error curves across days even when Day 1 feels clumsy and unmemorable.

When a tough physical skill — typing, surgery, tennis, piano — feels clumsy on Tuesday afternoon, trust Henry's curve: sleep on it, pick up the tool Wednesday morning, and watch your cerebellum remember what your conscious mind barely noticed.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **declarative memory ('Knowing What')**: conscious memory for facts and life episodes ('what I ate today', 'when we met') requiring the hippocampus to consolidate — comparable to your searchable mental journal.
- **procedural memory ('Knowing How')**: unconscious motor and habit skill ('riding a bike', 'mirror-star tracing') stored in basal ganglia, motor cortex, and cerebellum — comparable to muscle-circuit autopilot that improves across days without needing journal entries.
- **Mirror-Star Tracing Task**: tracing inside a narrow double-bordered 5-pointed star while viewing your hand ONLY in a vertical mirror behind an occlusion shield — comparable to Milner's ingenious puzzle where errors drop from ~30 to ~1 across 3 days.
- **anterograde amnesia**: inability to consolidate new conscious declarative memories after bilateral hippocampal damage — comparable to living in an intelligent, polite present without saving new chapters.
- **systems consolidation**: gradual transfer of episodic memories from temporary hippocampal indexing into long-term neocortical networks — comparable to why H.M. kept his 1930s childhood despite losing his hippocampi in 1953.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Hopping onto a bicycle ten years after your last ride: you couldn't write an essay on handlebar torque angles ('Knowing What'), yet your cerebellum balances effortlessly in two seconds ('Knowing How').
- Trying yourself to trace a 5-pointed star while looking ONLY in a bathroom mirror — feeling your hand fight backwards up-down physics on Trial 1.
- Honoring Brenda Milner (still sharp past 100!) whose handmade mirror-star box overturned centuries of single-memory dogma.
