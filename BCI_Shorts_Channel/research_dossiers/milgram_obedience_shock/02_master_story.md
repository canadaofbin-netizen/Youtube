# Stanley Milgram's obedience experiment — Master Story (longform depth)

Target: 8–15 minute documentary. Every number below is quoted as reported; source ids refer to `01_sources.json`.

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

New Haven, Connecticut, 1961–62. In a basement-quiet laboratory at Yale, a middle-aged man in a suit sits in front of a grey metal panel. Thirty lever switches run across it in a single line, from 15 volts on the left to 450 volts on the right, in 15-volt steps. Group labels read Slight Shock, Moderate Shock, Strong Shock, Very Strong Shock, Intense Shock, Extreme Intensity Shock, Danger: Severe Shock; the last two switches simply say XXX (SRC1). Each time he pushes a lever down, a red pilot light comes on, a buzzer sounds, a blue "voltage energizer" light flashes, the needle of the voltmeter swings to the right and relays click (SRC1). Behind him sits a man in a grey technician's coat. Through the wall, a man he met twenty minutes earlier is strapped into a chair. The volunteer hesitates, turns round, and hears a firm, polite voice: "Please continue." Then: "The experiment requires that you continue" (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

The experiments began in August 1961, three months after the trial of Adolf Eichmann opened in Jerusalem (SRC10). Milgram's own paper opens with the Holocaust: such policies "could only be carried out on a massive scale if a very large number of persons obeyed orders" (SRC1). Gina Perry adds that Milgram was also thinking about the "brainwashing" of American prisoners in the Korean War, and that he believed Hannah Arendt's picture of Eichmann as a cog in a bureaucratic machine applied to Americans in New Haven too (SRC8, NPR interview). The project was funded by the National Science Foundation, with 1960 pilot studies paid for by Yale's Higgins Fund (SRC1).

## 3. People

- **Stanley Milgram** — young Yale social psychologist who designed the apparatus and the script (SRC1).
- **The experimenter** — a 31-year-old high-school biology teacher, impassive and somewhat stern, in a grey technician's coat (SRC1).
- **The learner** — a 47-year-old accountant of Irish-American stock, trained for the role, whom most observers found mild-mannered and likable (SRC1).
- **The teachers** — 40 men aged 20 to 50 from New Haven and surrounding towns: postal clerks, high-school teachers, salesmen, engineers, labourers; education from unfinished elementary school to doctorates. Paid $4.50 "simply for coming to the laboratory" (SRC1).

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

1. **The cover story.** Two "volunteers" are told the study is about the effect of punishment on learning (SRC1).
2. **The rigged draw.** They draw slips from a hat. Both slips say "Teacher"; the confederate claims "Learner" (SRC1).
3. **Strapping in.** In the adjacent room the learner is strapped into an "electric chair" apparatus; an electrode is fixed to his wrist with paste "to avoid blisters and burns". The experimenter says the shocks "can be extremely painful" but "cause no permanent tissue damage" (SRC1).
4. **The sample shock.** Back at the generator, the teacher receives a real 45-volt shock on his wrist from the third switch, powered by a 45-volt battery hidden in the machine (SRC1).
5. **The task.** The teacher reads word pairs over the wall; the learner answers by pressing one of four switches that light a numbered quadrant in an answer box on top of the generator. For each wrong answer the teacher must announce the voltage and move one switch higher (SRC1).
6. **The protests.** In the 1963 baseline the learner says nothing until 300 volts, then pounds on the wall; he pounds again at 315 volts; after that there are no more answers (SRC1). In the later "new baseline" (Experiment 5) the learner mentions heart trouble, shouts at 150 volts that he wants to be let out, screams at higher levels and falls silent after 330 volts (SRC3).
7. **The prods.** When the teacher balks, the experimenter uses four prods in order: "Please continue" / "Please go on"; "The experiment requires that you continue"; "It is absolutely essential that you continue"; "You have no other choice, you must go on." After the fourth, the session ends. Special answers covered injury ("no permanent tissue damage, so please go on") and the learner's wishes ("Whether the learner likes it or not, you must go on") (SRC1).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

- Prediction: fourteen Yale psychology seniors expected 0 to 3 of 100 people to go to 450 volts; mean 1.2 percent (SRC1).
- Reality: no one stopped before 300 volts. Five stopped at 300, four at 315, two at 330, one each at 345, 360 and 375. Fourteen defied; **26 of 40 (65 percent) went to 450 volts** (SRC1).
- Belief check: asked how painful the last shocks were, the modal answer was 14 ("Extremely painful") on a 14-point scale, mean 13.42 (SRC1).
- Stress: men sweated, trembled, stuttered, bit their lips, groaned and dug fingernails into their flesh. Fourteen showed nervous laughter; three had "full-blown, uncontrollable seizures" of it — one, a 46-year-old encyclopedia salesman, so violent the session was stopped (SRC1). One observer watched a poised businessman become "a twitching, stuttering wreck" within 20 minutes, pulling his earlobe and muttering "Oh God, let's stop it" — and still obey to the end (SRC1).
- After the last shock, obedient men sighed, mopped their brows, rubbed their eyes or fumbled for cigarettes (SRC1).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

- **Not one number but many.** Milgram ran around 30 variants; obedience ranged from 0 to nearly 100 percent and, overall, most participants did not go to the end (SRC5). Same room as the learner: 40 percent. Forcing the learner's hand onto a shock plate: 30 percent (SRC2, SRC10). A plain office in Bridgeport with no Yale link: 47.5 percent (SRC10). Instructions by telephone: about 21 percent (SRC10). Two peer "teachers" refusing: only 4 of 40 continued (SRC10).
- **Orders failed; purpose worked.** People tended to continue when the experimenter appealed to the science ("The experiment requires that you continue") but typically refused the bare order "You have no other choice, you must go on" (SRC5, citing SRC6). Haslam and Reicher call this engaged followership, not blind obedience.
- **Ethics.** Diana Baumrind's 1964 critique attacked the deception and distress (SRC7).
- **Perry's archive.** Gina Perry, after interviewing former subjects and reading the files, argued that only about half fully believed the set-up, that sessions departed from the published script, and that some subjects were never properly told the truth; one defiant subject still resented, 50 years later, never being "de-hoaxed" (SRC8).

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Milgram's 1963 paper was followed by the 1965 Human Relations report on the proximity series (SRC2) and the 1974 book with 19 variations (SRC3). His black-and-white film *Obedience*, shot in May 1962, became the visual record (SRC12). Ethics boards later made a full replication impossible. In 2009 Jerry Burger ran a partial replication that stopped at 150 volts: 70 percent were willing to continue, against 82.5 percent in Milgram's comparable condition (SRC4). Reviews by Blass (SRC9) summarise the decades of replications. Milgram's original generator is held by the Archives of the History of American Psychology at the University of Akron (SRC10, SRC11).

## 8. What it means for you

The experiment is less a proof that people are monsters than a map of when good people go along: when a respected place and a calm voice frame harm as a worthy cause, when the victim is out of sight, and when no one else says no. Bring the person closer, strip away the official setting, or let one peer refuse, and obedience collapses. A bare order — "you have no other choice" — was the prod people resisted most. The useful lesson: when someone says "it's for a good reason, please continue", that is exactly the moment to pause and ask who is actually responsible.
