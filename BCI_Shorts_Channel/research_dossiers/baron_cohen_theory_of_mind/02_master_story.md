# Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie & Uta Frith (1985): The 'Sally-Anne' Marble Transfer & Theory of Mind — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

Sit across a wooden table from a bright three-year-old and act out a thirty-second play: Doll Sally puts a shiny marble in her basket and steps outside; while she's gone, Doll Anne moves the marble into a closed box (SRC1). Ask the child where the marble REALLY is, and they nail it — 'the box!' Now ask where Sally will LOOK first when she walks back in — and four out of five three-year-olds point straight to the box too, baffled that Sally doesn't magically know what their own eyes just saw.

Recorded in 1985, MRC Cognitive Development Unit testing table, London, the defining visual record centers on a 1985 tabletop developmental test with two small dolls, a round lidded wicker basket, a rectangular lidded white box, and a glass marble as a child points to the basket (SRC1, SRC_STILL). With two dolls, a basket, a box, and one moved marble in 1985, the Sally-Anne experiment revealed how human minds around age four learn to hold someone else's outdated belief separate from reality — a skill even busy adults lose under the 'Curse of Knowledge' (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

After Premack and Woodruff (1978) asked whether chimpanzees possess a 'Theory of Mind,' philosopher Daniel Dennett pointed out the acid test: only predicting behavior from a FALSE belief proves you are tracking someone's mind rather than just reporting reality — inspiring Wimmer & Perner (1983) and the ultra-clean 1985 Sally-Anne puppet stage.

At MRC Cognitive Development Unit, London in 1985, **Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith's 1985 'Sally-Anne' false-belief puppet experiment** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan M. Leslie, Uta Frith (1985). Does the autistic child have a “theory of mind” ? Cognition).

## 3. People

- **Simon Baron-Cohen**: Developmental psychopathologist who co-created the Sally-Anne puppet adaptation of Wimmer & Perner's false-belief task (SRC1).
- **Alan M. Leslie & Uta Frith**: MRC London cognitive scientists who co-theorized metarepresentation and decoupled mental-state tracking (SRC1).
- **Heinz Wimmer & Josef Perner**: Developmental psychologists who invented the original 1983 Maxi-and-the-chocolate false-belief paradigm (SRC1).

Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith collaborated at London's MRC Cognitive Development Unit in 1985; decades later autistic scholar Damian Morton/Milton ('Double Empathy Problem') and Morton Ann Gernsbacher reshaped how science interprets neurodiversity and perspective-taking.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Children watched the two-doll marble transfer and answered three questions: Belief ('Where will Sally look?'), Reality Control ('Where is the marble really?'), and Memory Control ('Where was it in the beginning?') (SRC1).

1. **Tabletop Sally-Anne puppet stage as Anne moves the glass marble from the round wicker basket into the closed rectangular box** (*1985, MRC Cognitive Development Unit testing table, London*). The scene unfolds inside a sunlit 1985 London developmental playroom with a clean wooden child-height table holding two small cloth-dressed dolls (Sally and Anne), one round woven wicker basket with a lid on the left, one crisp white rectangular cardboard box with a lid on the right, and a gleaming blue-swirl glass marble. The researcher (wearing 1985 knit cardigan) is seated across the low wooden table moving Doll Anne's hands to transfer the glass marble from the round wicker basket into the rectangular box while Doll Sally rests out of sight below the table edge; Four-Year-Old Child (wearing 1985 corduroy jumper) are watching intently across the table, then pointing one clear index finger back to Sally's round wicker basket when Sally returns. The experimental setup features **round woven wicker basket (Sally's)** (small lidded tan basket on the left side of the table — empty after the secret swap; correctly pointed to by 4.5-year-olds answering 'Where will Sally LOOK?'), **rectangular lidded box (Anne's) & glass marble** (crisp white lidded box on the right holding the secretly transferred blue glass marble; pointed to by 3-year-olds swayed by reality bias), and **two small cloth-dressed tabletop dolls (Sally & Anne)** (simple matte-/cloth study puppets in red vs blue aprons; stage the exit, secret transfer, and return cleanly). The doll Sally places the glass marble into the round wicker basket and exits below the table rim, and then doll Anne lifts the marble across into the rectangular box and closes both lids. The doll Sally returns researcher asks 'Where will Sally look?', and then 4-year-old Child points steadily to the round wicker basket. In response, Four-Year-Old Child smiles knowingly, holding BOTH facts cleanly: marble is in the box, yet Sally believes basket; the researcher checks off all three items: Belief Question + Reality Control + Memory Control. You just watched Anne hide the marble in the white box — yet predicting Sally will still check her empty wicker basket requires holding two conflicting worlds inside one head (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **Age 3 reality pull vs Age 4.5 perspective leap** (*1985, London developmental playroom*). Set within a side-by-side tabletop moment comparing a 3-year-old pointing eagerly to the white box (where the marble really sits) vs a 4.5-year-old pointing to the wicker basket. Three-Year-Old Child are pointing straight at Anne's rectangular box despite passing both memory controls. Central to the procedure is **178-study developmental curve card** (clean trajectory rising sharply from age 3.0 to 4.5; illustrates universal childhood maturation). Three-Year-Old Child points immediately to the real marble location in the box. Immediately afterward, the researcher notes intact memory paired with pre-decoupled reality bias. At age three, what YOU know feels like what the whole universe knows (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **Modern Double Empathy & Adult 'Tap-the-Song' Curse of Knowledge** (*Modern university communication lab*). The setup centers on a bright seminar table where one adult taps knuckles rhythmically on oak expecting the listener across the table to recognize 'Happy Birthday' — while the listener hears only random knocks. The tapper is tapping desk rhythmically confident the melody is obvious; the listener is tilting head puzzled hearing disconnected wooden taps. Central to the procedure is **50% Predicted vs 2.5% Actual recognition placard** (Elizabeth Newton's classic adult Curse of Knowledge score; proves healthy adults make Sally-Anne errors daily). The tapper taps song, then listener guesses wrong, and finally tapper laughs realizing the orchestra only played inside their own head. Across the room, both bridge the two-way perspective gap by naming the context aloud. Adults guess listeners hear a tapped song 50% of the time — actual score is 2.5%, proving Sally-Anne blind spots never fully retire (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **Adding the missing 'marble moved' sentence before hitting Send** (*Modern office desk*). The setup centers on a bright desk monitor where a writer catches an ambiguous message and adds one crisp context line ('Context: yesterday we moved the budget file from Folder A to Folder B'). The writer is pausing over keyboard to add one clear background sentence for a colleague who missed the meeting. Central to the procedure is **clean screen message with explicit 'Context:' line** (bridges the unseen transfer; eliminates three rounds of confusion). The writer types the missing context line, and then sends clear message. At that moment, the colleague across room gives an immediate thumbs-up understanding on the first read. One sentence telling your reader when the marble moved cures the Curse of Knowledge (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Every group passed Reality and Memory controls, proving zero amnesia. Between ages 3 and 4.5, typical children leap from ~20% to ~85% false-belief accuracy (confirmed across 178 global studies), while clinical timing varied with language scaffolding (SRC1, SRC2).

- Building on Premack & Woodruff (1978) and Wimmer & Perner's 1983 chocolate story, Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith created the minimalist 'Sally-Anne' tabletop doll test at London's MRC Cognitive Development Unit in 1985. (SRC1)
- The child sits across from two small dolls: Sally (who has a round covered basket) and Anne (who has a rectangular covered box). (SRC1)
- Sally places a glass marble inside her basket and walks out of the room; while Sally is GONE, mischievous Anne lifts the marble out of the basket and hides it inside Anne's own closed box. (SRC1)
- Before asking the test question, researchers verify two control questions ('Where is the marble REALLY right now?' — Box; 'Where was the marble in the beginning?' — Basket) — passed 100 percent by every group. (SRC1)
- Then Sally returns, and the researcher asks the decisive Belief Question: 'Where will Sally LOOK for her marble?'. (SRC1)
- Passing requires holding TWO contradictory realities in your head at once: YOU know the marble is in the box, yet SALLY falsely believes it is still in her basket because she didn't see the swap. (SRC1)
- Across typical developmental studies (Wellman et al. 2001 meta-analysis of 178 studies), 3-year-olds reliably fail verbal false-belief questions (~80% point to the box where the marble really is!), whereas by age 4.5 roughly 85 percent pass effortlessly (23 of 27 typical 4-year-olds and 12 of 14 children with Down syndrome of lower verbal mental age passed in 1985, while 16 of 20 autistic children in that high-support 1985 clinical sample pointed to the box). (SRC1)
- Crucial modern update (Gernsbacher & Yergeau 2019; Milton's Double Empathy Problem; Baron-Cohen 2025): eye-tracking and modern replications proved 1) many autistic youths and adults pass verbal false-belief tasks cleanly depending on language load; 2) non-autistic people struggle JUST as hard to read autistic expressions ('two-way Double Empathy mismatch', not one-way brokenness); and 3) EVERY healthy adult routinely slips into the exact same 'Curse of Knowledge' error when writing confusing emails. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Ask a bright three-year-old 'Where will Sally look for her marble?' right after Anne secretly moved it, and the three-year-old points straight to the new box — completely unable to imagine Sally doesn't know what THEY just saw — a striking result given that separating 'what is true' from 'what someone else believes' is a developmental leap between ages 3 and 5 (SRC1). Every child in the room answered 'Where is the marble REALLY?' and 'Where did it start?' at 100 percent accuracy — the ONLY hurdle was decoupling someone else's unseen mental model from reality — notable because memory and eyesight were spotless; perspective decoupling was the puzzle (SRC1). Modern researchers (Damian Milton; Gernsbacher) overturned 1990s 'universal mindblindness' overgeneralizations by testing the reverse direction: neurotypical observers misread autistic people's emotions and intentions at high rates too ('the Double Empathy Problem') — revealing how misunderstanding across different minds runs on a two-way street (SRC1). Even PhD adults fail adult versions of Sally-Anne every single day — tapping out 'Happy Birthday' on a table and guessing listeners hear the tune 50 percent of the time when actual listeners guess just 2.5 percent ('Curse of Knowledge') — highlighting that once you know where the marble is, forgetting you know it takes effort at any age (SRC1).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Two crucial modern corrections transformed how we read 1985 (SRC1): first, sweeping 1990s claims of universal 'autistic mindblindness' failed replication — verbal complexity heavily drove test gaps, empathy/care remained deep, and Damian Milton proved neurotypical people struggle JUST as badly at reading autistic expressions ('the Double Empathy Problem' — a two-way translation gap, not a one-way defect!). Second, Elizabeth Newton's 'song-tapping' studies proved EVERY adult still fails Sally-Anne daily whenever the 'Curse of Knowledge' tricks us into assuming coworkers see what's inside our own heads.

- **"Three-year-olds point to Anne's box because they forgot where Sally put the marble."** — They pass the 'Where was it at the beginning?' memory check 100% of the time; reality-bias overrides false-belief decoupling. (SRC1)
- **"Every autistic person lacks empathy or fails Theory of Mind tasks."** — That outdated stereotype collapsed under empirical scrutiny (SRC1): affective care/empathy is distinct from rapid social cueing, language demands heavily skew Sally-Anne scores, and misunderstanding between neurotypes is bidirectional ('Double Empathy'). (SRC1)
- **"Once you turn five, you never confuse your own knowledge with someone else's again."** — Adults constantly fall for the 'Curse of Knowledge' — writing cryptic acronyms or directions assuming readers see the map inside the writer's head. (SRC1)
- **"Theory of Mind is a single binary switch you either have or don't."** — Mentalizing spans graded skills from eye-gaze tracking to second-order beliefs ('Alice thinks Bob thinks...') and taxing tired adult prefrontal resources. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

False-belief research mapped the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) mentalizing network, improved pediatric language support, and upgraded technical writing and two-way cross-neurotype communication.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Simon Baron-Cohen (2011). At the neural-circuit level, **parietal cortex** (temporoparietal perspective hub (TPJ)) contributes by decoupling another person's outdated belief ('Sally still thinks basket') from your own visual knowledge ('marble is in box'), which connects pointing back to Sally's wicker basket to temporoparietal false-belief decoupling; meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex** (reality-inhibition brake) contributes by suppressing the automatic urge to blurt out where the marble REALLY sits right now, which explains why 3-year-olds (and hurried adults) default to their own current knowledge.

## 8. What it means for you

With two dolls, a basket, a box, and one moved marble in 1985, the Sally-Anne experiment revealed how human minds around age four learn to hold someone else's outdated belief separate from reality — a skill even busy adults lose under the 'Curse of Knowledge.' Whenever you explain an idea or resolve a misunderstanding, deliberately pause and ask: 'Did this other person actually see the marble move, or is that context only inside MY head?'

Before blaming someone for 'missing the obvious,' check two things: 1) were they actually in the room when Anne moved the marble? and 2) are you both speaking different communication styles that need two-way translation?

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **Theory of Mind (mentalizing)**: tracking that other people hold beliefs, memories, and perspectives different from your own current knowledge — like keeping separate save-files for what each character in a mystery novel knows so far.
- **false-belief task**: testing whether an observer predicts someone will act on their outdated belief rather than a secret change they didn't witness — like knowing a surprise party hides behind the door while predicting your friend expects an empty kitchen.
- **Curse of Knowledge**: adult cognitive bias where knowing a fact makes it brutally hard to simulate what a newcomer doesn't know — like tapping a rhythm on a desk and hearing the whole orchestra in your own head.
- **Double Empathy Problem**: modern finding (Milton) that communication mismatches between neurodivergent and neurotypical people stem from mutual differences in style, not a one-sided deficit — like two fluent speakers of different dialects needing shared translation.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Sending a three-word Slack message ('Check the doc!') assuming your teammate knows which of forty docs you just had open ('adult Sally-Anne error').
- Watching a 3-year-old play hide-and-seek by covering ONLY their own eyes in the middle of the rug ('if I can't see you, you can't see me').
- Practicing 'Double Empathy' humility: asking directly what a colleague meant instead of assuming everyone signals feelings with identical body language.
- Reading a draft email pretending you do NOT know the secret move Anne made in Paragraph 3.
