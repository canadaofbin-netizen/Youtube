# Lawrence Weiskrantz (1974): 'Blindsight' — Patient D.B. Pointing With 90% Accuracy Into Total Conscious Darkness — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 1973 at London's famous Queen Square neurology hospital, a young man known as **Patient D.B.** underwent life-changing brain surgery to remove a benign tangle of vessels in his right primary visual cortex (**V1**). Surgery cured his brutal migraines and left the entire left half of his visual world consciously pitch-dark. Every standard eye chart confirmed it: **100% blind on the left**. Then Oxford psychologist Lawrence Weiskrantz walked in with a strange request: *'I know you see nothing on your left — pretend you're flipping a coin and point anyway!'* D.B. laughed, reached into total blackness, and hit silent bullseyes **over 90% of the time** (SRC1, SRC2).

Recorded in 1974, National Hospital, Queen Square, London, the defining visual record centers on a 1974 clinical testing room where a seated patient on a chin-rest stares straight ahead while pointing accurately at a silent target on the left half of a curved white perimeter screen beside a 93% accuracy clipboard (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 1974 at London's Queen Square hospital, Lawrence Weiskrantz and Elizabeth Warrington discovered 'Blindsight' — proving Patient D.B. could point to silent targets and tell 'X' from 'O' at over 90% accuracy inside a completely blind visual field via ancient subcortical brain pathways that bypass conscious visual cortex (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

For over a century after Hermann Munk and WWI occipital-wound studies, neurology equated primary visual cortex (V1) with 100% of human vision — if V1 was gone, eyes might as well be unplugged on that side.

At National Hospital for Nervous Diseases, Queen Square, London & University of Oxford in 1974, **Lawrence Weiskrantz, Elizabeth Warrington, M. D. Sanders & J. Marshall's 1974 Patient D.B. 'Blindsight' discovery** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: L. Weiskrantz, Elizabeth K. Warrington, M. D. Sanders, J. Marshall (1974). VISUAL CAPACITY IN THE HEMIANOPIC FIELD FOLLOWING A RESTRICTED OCCIPITAL ABLATION. Brain).

## 3. People

- **Lawrence Weiskrantz**: Oxford experimental psychologist who coined the word 'Blindsight' in 1974 after persuading clinically blind Patient D.B. to 'just guess' into his dark visual field (SRC1).
- **Patient 'D.B.'**: Young British man whose right primary visual cortex (V1 / calcarine cortex) was surgically removed at Queen Square to cure a benign vascular malformation triggering severe migraines — leaving his left visual field consciously pitch-dark (SRC1).
- **Elizabeth K. Warrington & Nicholas Humphrey**: Queen Square neuropsychologist co-discoverer and Cambridge primate researcher whose monkey 'Helen' inspired Weiskrantz to test human hand-pointing inside blind fields (SRC1).

Inspired by Cambridge researcher Nicholas Humphrey (who spent years rehabilitating a V1-lesioned monkey named Helen until she caught moving currants!), Queen Square neuropsychologist Elizabeth Warrington and Oxford's Lawrence Weiskrantz (1926-2018) teamed up with Patient D.B., coining the immortal word **'Blindsight'** in *The Lancet* in 1974 (SRC1).

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Keeping D.B.'s eyes strictly fixed straight ahead on a perimeter cross (and checking the natural optic-disc blind spot to rule out stray light scatter!), Weiskrantz et al. (1974, SRC1) flashed silent spots, X's, O's, and line gratings inside his blind left hemifield under two-alternative forced-choice guessing and manual reaching.

1. **1974 Queen Square Perimeter Arc: Patient D.B. Fixing Straight Ahead While Pointing Dead-Center Into His 'Blind' Left Field** (*1974, National Hospital, Queen Square, London*). The scene unfolds inside a sunlit 1974 British hospital neuro-ophthalmology testing room featuring a curved white semicircular perimeter screen with a central fixation cross; on the right ('Intact Field') and left ('Consciously Blind Left Field') sit silent optical projection spots and 'X vs O' plaques, alongside a scorecard reading 'Conscious Sight: 0% | Forced-Choice Pointing: 92%'. Patient D.B. (wearing 1974 knit V-neck jumper over collared shirt) is chin resting steadily on the white perimeter chin-rest, eyes locked strictly on the center cross, extending his index finger to touch the silent light spot in his 'blind' left arc while shaking his head saying he saw nothing; Lawrence Weiskrantz & Elizabeth Warrington (wearing 1974 white clinical coats) are silently projecting a silent target and 'X / O' grating into the left arc, then turning the 92%-accurate scorecard around to show the astonished patient. The experimental setup features **curved white clinical perimeter arc with central fixation cross** (separates D.B.'s intact right field from his V1-lesioned blind left field; exact 1974 Queen Square clinical apparatus), **silent light-spot projector & 'X vs O' orientation paddles** (zero sound cues — pure silent visual targets inside the blind hemifield; tests location pointing AND shape orientation), and **'Seen: 0 / 30 — Pointed Accurately: 28 / 30 (93%)' reveal clipboard** (captures D.B.'s own famous disbelief when shown his hit map; delivers the core human astonishment beat). Lawrence Weiskrantz flashes a silent target spot 20 degrees inside D.B.'s blind left arc and prompts 'Just guess with your finger', and then patient D.B. reaches smoothly and touches dead-center on the spot while reporting 'Nothing there'. Elizabeth Warrington turns the cluster-dotted target map around, and then patient D.B. leans back in amazement seeing 28 bullseyes. In response, Patient D.B stares wide-eyed at his own bullseye target sheet ('I swear it was pure guesswork!'); Weiskrantz & Warrington record the birth of 'Blindsight' for Brain and The Lancet. He swears on his life the left half of the screen is empty — then puts his finger bullseye on 28 out of 30 silent targets (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **2008 Corridor Obstacle Course Without V1 (Patient T.N.)** (*2008, European university neurology corridor*). Set within a bright sunlit hospital corridor scattered with low wooden chairs, cardboard boxes, and paper-roll obstacles. The cortically blind Patient T.N. participant is walking unassisted down the bright corridor side-stepping every chair and box cleanly without a cane or collision; two neurologist are trailing safely a step behind with dropped jaws and clipboards. Central to the procedure is **staggered hallway chairs & boxes** (navigated with zero collisions despite bilateral V1 loss; de Gelder et al. 2008 landmark video proof). Patient T.N. weaves left around a chair and steps smoothly past a box while reporting zero conscious sight. Immediately afterward, the neurologists applaud quietly at intact subcortical dorsal navigation. Zero primary visual cortex in either hemisphere — yet his ancient midbrain radar steers his feet cleanly around every chair in the hall (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **Affective Blindsight: Catching Fear Without Seeing a Face** (*2009, Tilburg / Torino affective neuroscience lab*). This phase takes place in a clean testing booth flashing a fearful vs smiling portrait into the blind field while tiny cheek sensors record involuntary mimicry. Blindsight participant is reporting an empty screen while their brow subtly knits on unseen fear cards and relaxes on unseen smile cards. Central to the procedure is **facial expression cards & amygdala-colliculus pathway diagram** (proves unseen emotions reach amygdala via superior colliculus; Tamietto & de Gelder 2010). The experimenter flashes unseen smile card, and then participant's zygomatic cheek trace lifts automatically. Across the room, the experimenter notes intact subcortical emotion radar. Even when conscious sight is pitch-dark, an unseen smile or frightened face reaches your amygdala and moves your own cheek muscles (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **Why You Still Need Consciousness: The Untouched Glass of Water** (*1974 / Philosophical synthesis scene*). The setup centers on a sunlit table where a clear glass of water sits in the blind left field — untouched until someone prompts a guess. The patient is feeling thirsty yet never spontaneously reaching left until turning head to bring the glass into conscious sight. Central to the procedure is **clear water glass on left vs right side of table** (illustrates what unconscious blindsight CANNOT do: initiate spontaneous self-chosen goals; debunks 'consciousness is useless' myth). The patient ignores glass in blind zone, then turns eyes into intact conscious field, and finally smiles and picks up glass immediately. At that moment, the patient drinks purposefully once conscious V1 engages. Blindsight can dodge a chair or win a forced guess — ONLY conscious sight lets you look at a glass of water and choose to drink (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

While sincerely reporting **zero visual awareness** on trial after trial, D.B. pointed accurately across 30° of his blind field and discriminated X vs O and horizontal vs vertical gratings at **85% to >95% accuracy** — gasping in sheer disbelief when shown his own scorecard (SRC1, SRC2).

- Standard neurology before 1974 taught a simple rule: destroy human primary visual cortex (**V1** at the back of the occipital lobe), and the opposite half of space goes permanently, 100% blind — because physicians only ever asked *'Tell me when you SEE the light'*. (SRC1)
- Inspired by Nicholas Humphrey's monkey 'Helen' (who caught moving currants without V1), Elizabeth Warrington and Lawrence Weiskrantz tested **Patient D.B.** at Queen Square in 1973-1974 after surgical removal of his right V1 left his left visual field completely blind on standard hospital perimetry. (SRC1)
- Instead of asking 'Do you see anything?' (to which D.B. always truthfully answered **'Nothing at all — zero!'**), Weiskrantz changed the rules to **forced-choice guessing**: *'Reach out and touch where a target MIGHT be'* or *'Just guess like flipping a coin: was it an X or an O, horizontal or vertical lines?'*. (SRC1)
- D.B. laughed that guessing in total blackness was ridiculous — yet across dozens of trials his finger landed **almost dead-center on silent light spots across 30 degrees of his 'blind' field**, and he discriminated **X vs O and line orientations at 85% to >95% accuracy** while insisting he saw NOTHING. (SRC1)
- When Weiskrantz finally showed D.B. his own scorecard in *Brain* / *The Lancet* (1974), D.B. was utterly dumbstruck: *'Did I really do that? I didn't see a thing — it was pure guesswork!'*. (SRC1)
- Careful controls ruled out stray light bouncing onto D.B.'s good half-retina (flashing targets onto the natural optic-disc blind spot inside the blind field dropped accuracy right down to 50% chance!). (SRC1)
- How did D.B.'s hand know what 'he' didn't see? About 10% of optic-nerve fibers bypass V1 completely, branching down to the ancient midbrain **superior colliculus**, **pulvinar**, and lateral geniculate shortcuts straight into dorsal action/motion areas (MT/V5) — guiding eyes and hands WITHOUT switching on conscious awareness. (SRC2)
- Decades later, Beatrice de Gelder and Marco Tamietto discovered **'Affective Blindsight'** in bilateral-V1-stroke patient **T.N.**: completely cortically blind across BOTH eyes, T.N. walked down a long corridor weaving cleanly around chairs, boxes, and tripods without a single collision — AND unconsciously flinched/smiled in facial EMG when shown unseen fearful vs happy faces via the superior-colliculus to amygdala shortcut. (SRC3)
- In Weiskrantz et al.'s (1974) forced-choice reaching and grating orientation trials, Patient D.B. reported zero conscious sight in his scotoma yet exceeded 90% accuracy — reaching roughly 93% on spatial localization blocks. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Ask Patient D.B. what he sees on his left and he swears on his life: **'Pitch black — nothing!'** Tell him 'Just point anyway,' and his finger lands dead on the silent target 9 times out of 10 — leaving D.B. gasping when shown his own chart — a striking result given that seeing and consciously KNOWING you see are two separate systems in the human brain (SRC1). In 2008, a doctor with strokes in BOTH visual cortices (Patient T.N.) — clinically blind across his entire visual world — put down his white cane and zig-zagged cleanly around trash cans, chairs, and boxes down a hospital hallway without touching a single obstacle — notable because your ancient midbrain obstacle radar works even when conscious sight is dark (SRC3). Show an angry or terrified face inside a blindsight field: the patient swears nobody was on the display, yet their amygdala lights up and their own cheek/brow muscles unconsciously mirror the emotion — revealing how 'affective Blindsight': catching human emotion without conscious sight (SRC3). For a hundred years doctors missed Blindsight simply because they asked *'Raise your hand IF you see it'* instead of *'Guess left or right even when you see nothing'* — highlighting that changing one question unlocked the neuroscience of consciousness (SRC1).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

How can a blind hand outsmart a conscious human mind? Evolution built TWO visual highways: the high-definition conscious highway through **V1**, and an ancient ~10% midbrain bypass through the **superior colliculus and pulvinar** straight to parietal action maps and the amygdala. In 2008, Beatrice de Gelder filmed Patient T.N. (missing V1 on BOTH sides!) weaving cleanly around a hallway full of chairs and boxes without a single bump (SRC3). Yet Blindsight also proved why consciousness MATTERS: left alone while thirsty, blindsight patients never spontaneously reach for water sitting in their blind zone — unconscious vision reacts when prodded, while **conscious vision empowers self-directed choice**.

- **"Patients with Blindsight are secretly peeking or seeing blurry dim shadows."** — In 'Type 1 Blindsight', subjective visibility is genuinely zero even on high-confidence betting scales — which is why blindsight patients can starve in front of a glass of water on their blind side unless explicitly prompted by someone else to 'guess'. (SRC1)
- **"Unconscious blindsight proves conscious awareness is a useless decoration ('epiphenomenon')."** — Just the opposite. Without conscious V1 sight, Patient D.B. NEVER spontaneously reached for a cup or initiated plans toward objects in his blind field — consciousness is what lets you act *on your own* without an experimenter prompting 'guess now!'. (SRC1)
- **"Only humans have two visual streams."** — Evolution built the ancient midbrain collicular 'Where is it moving?' radar in fish, frogs, and mammals hundreds of millions of years before expanding primate V1 high-definition conscious cortex. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Weiskrantz's discovery turned consciousness from taboo philosophy into hard experimental neuroscience, inspiring cortical-blindness rehabilitation and two-stream models of perception and action.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC5** (P Stoerig (1997). Blindsight in man and monkey. Brain) offered a comprehensive physiological and behavioral synthesis of blindsight across human V1 lesions and primate models. **SRC6** (Sara Ajina, Franco Pestilli, Ariel Rokem, Christopher Kennard et al. (2015). Human blindsight is mediated by an intact geniculo-extrastriate pathway. eLife) provided Ajina, Pestilli, Rokem, Kennard & Bridge (2015 eLife) diffusion tractography proof that human blindsight depends on intact lateral geniculate to hMT+/V5 pathways. **SRC7** (Beatrice de Gelder, Marco Tamietto, Geert van Boxtel, Rainer Goebel et al. (2008). Intact navigation skills after bilateral loss of striate cortex. Current Biology) presented a de Gelder et al. (2008 Current Biology) obstacle-corridor navigation demonstration in complete bilateral cortical blindness (Patient TN). Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Lawrence Weiskrantz (1992). UNCONSCIOUS VISION. The Sciences (**SRC2**); M. Jeannerod (1988). Blindsight. A case study and implicationsL. Weiskrantz. Oxford Psychology Series, No. 12, Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1986. Neuropsychologia (**SRC3**); and D. N. Rushton (1987). Blindsight: A Case Study and Implications. By L. Weiskrantz. (Pp. 187; illustrated; £19.50.) Clarendon Press: Oxford. 1986. Psychological Medicine (**SRC4**).

At the neural-circuit level, **primary visual cortex** (lesioned conscious sight screen (right V1)) contributes by darkening conscious awareness across the left visual field so Patient D.B. truthfully reports seeing nothing at all, which establishes why standard clinical perimetry scored D.B. as 100% blind on the left; meanwhile, the **optic nerve** (10% subcortical midbrain bypass) contributes by routing intact retinal signals past damaged V1 directly into midbrain/thalamic relays (superior colliculus & pulvinar) and dorsal parietal action maps, which explains how location and orientation survive without V1; meanwhile, **parietal cortex** (unconscious hand-pointing & obstacle guidance) contributes by guiding D.B.'s index finger dead-center onto the silent light target at >90% accuracy while conscious speech still says 'pure guess', which links subcortical-dorsal bypass to the iconic 1974 pointing score.

## 8. What it means for you

In 1974 at London's Queen Square hospital, Lawrence Weiskrantz and Elizabeth Warrington discovered 'Blindsight' — proving Patient D.B. could point to silent targets and tell 'X' from 'O' at over 90% accuracy inside a completely blind visual field via ancient subcortical brain pathways that bypass conscious visual cortex. Trust rapid peripheral dodge reflexes when driving or walking, while appreciating what conscious vision actually gives you: the power to reflect, choose, and initiate purposeful action without waiting for an external prompt.

Every time your hand snatches a falling keys-ring or your feet dodge a curb before you consciously 'see' it, you are thanking your ancient midbrain blindsight highway.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **Blindsight**: accurate pointing, motion tracking, or X-vs-O guessing (>90%) inside a visual-field zone where V1 damage leaves zero conscious sight — comparable to a working radar screen in the cockpit when the main passenger cabin TV is unplugged.
- **Primary Visual Cortex (V1)**: entry hub at the back of the occipital lobe required for normal conscious visual experience — comparable to the screen where sight becomes 'I see that!'.
- **subcortical collicular shortcut**: ancient ~10% optic-nerve branch routing eye inputs through the midbrain superior colliculus and thalamus straight to action and emotion hubs — comparable to an emergency side-stairwell bypassing the closed main lobby.
- **forced-choice guessing**: asking a patient to choose 'X or O / point somewhere' on every trial even when they insist they see nothing — comparable to the simple lab question that revealed hidden unconscious perception.
- **Affective Blindsight**: unconsciously mirroring and reacting to fearful or happy faces flashed in a blind field via the colliculus-to-amygdala shortcut — comparable to catching a room's emotional vibe below conscious sight.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Ducking or jerking your hand back from a flying ball in peripheral vision half a second BEFORE your conscious mind registers what the object even was.
- Walking down stairs reading something else and catching a misstep automatically via dorsal/subcortical action vision.
- Realizing why 'having a gut hunch' sometimes reflects real sub-threshold sensory processing.
