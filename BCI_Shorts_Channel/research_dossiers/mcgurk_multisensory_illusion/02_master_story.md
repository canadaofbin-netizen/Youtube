# McGurk & MacDonald (1976): Hearing Voices Your Ears Never Received — How Vision Rewrites Speech ('BA' + 'GA' = 'DA') — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 1976 at the University of Surrey, developmental psychologist Harry McGurk asked a studio technician for a weird favor: take a film of a woman's face saying **'ga-ga'** and dub the sound **'ba-ba'** on top of it. When the technician handed back the tape, McGurk and colleague John MacDonald played it — and complained the technician had messed up and recorded **'DA-DA'**. Annoyed, McGurk shut his eyes to listen closer — and instantly heard **'BA-BA'**. He opened his eyes: **'DA-DA'**. Shut them: **'BA-BA'** (SRC1). By pure studio accident, they had stumbled onto one of the greatest illusions in brain science.

Recorded in 1976, University of Surrey Audiovisual Psychology Suite, Guildford, UK, the defining visual record centers on a 1976 researcher in an audiovisual lab sliding a white paddle across a video monitor showing 'GA' lips beside a desktop loudspeaker playing 'BA' while a seated subject flips between 'DA' and 'BA' cards (SRC1, SRC_STILL). Discovered by accident in a 1976 British dubbing room, the McGurk Effect proved that 98% of adults hear a brand-new third sound ('DA') when 'BA' audio meets 'GA' lip movements — because the temporal lobe fuses sight and sound before conscious hearing even forms (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Textbooks treated speech perception as a strictly acoustic pipeline running from ear cochlea to auditory cortex, assuming vision only stepped in as an emergency crutch for profound deafness.

At Department of Psychology, University of Surrey, Guildford, UK in 1976, **Harry McGurk & John MacDonald's 1976 Nature 'Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices' dubbing experiment** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Harry Mcgurk, John Macdonald (1976). Hearing lips and seeing voices. Nature).

## 3. People

- **Harry McGurk & John MacDonald**: Developmental psychologists studying infant perception at Surrey who asked a technician to dub mismatched syllables onto video — and couldn't believe their own ears (SRC1).
- **Technician in the Surrey AV suite**: Created the split audio-video dub ('ba-ba' sound onto 'ga-ga' lip movements) that fooled both researchers into thinking the audio track had been recorded wrong (SRC1).
- **Michael S. Beauchamp & Nath**: Neuroscientists who localized human McGurk audiovisual fusion to the posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus (pSTS) and proved TMS disruption of pSTS turns 'da' right back into 'ba' (SRC2).

Harry McGurk (1936-1998) and graduate colleague John MacDonald published their crisp 3-page paper 'Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices' in Nature just before Christmas 1976; decades later Michael Beauchamp's team mapped its exact cortical hub with fMRI and TMS.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

McGurk and MacDonald (1976, SRC1) tested 103 preschoolers, grade-schoolers, and adults under two conditions — watching and listening to dubbed 'ba-voice / ga-lips' (and 'ga-voice / ba-lips') videos versus listening to the identical audio track while facing away.

1. **1976 Surrey AV Studio: Covering the Monitor Screen Flips 'DA-DA' Instantly Back to 'BA-BA'** (*1976, University of Surrey Audiovisual Psychology Suite, Guildford, UK*). Viewed in a sunlit 1976 British university audiovisual control room with a crisp wooden-cabinet studio monitor displaying a close-up face pronouncing open-mouth 'GA-GA', a freestanding desktop loudspeaker cone beside acoustic waveform meters consistently reading 'BA-BA', and an opaque white occlusion paddle used to cover and uncover the screen. Harry McGurk (wearing 1976 British tweed jacket and wide-lapel shirt) is holding a white cardstock screen-cover paddle inches in front of the studio monitor — pulling it aside ('98% Hear DA!') then sliding it back ('100% Hear BA!'); John MacDonald & Adult Subjects are leaning toward the loudspeaker in disbelief as closing their eyes or blocking the screen switches what their ears hear on an unchanged audio loop. The experimental setup features **1976 studio video monitor ('Visual Lips: GA') paired with desktop loudspeaker ('Audio Wave: BA')** (side-by-side display + speaker making the conflicting sensory inputs crystal clear; documents the exact 1976 dubbing accident), and **sliding white 'Eyes Open = DA / Screen Covered = BA' test paddle** (simple hand-held occlusion board blocking ONLY the visual face while the speaker cone plays unobstructed; proves instantly that the sound room never changed the audio). Harry McGurk plays the repeating 'BA' loudspeaker loop with the monitor uncovered, then subject points confidently to the 'DA' response card, then McGurk slides the white paddle in front of the monitor screen without touching the speaker, and finally subject gasps and points immediately to 'BA'. In response, the adult subject opens and closes hands over eyes repeatedly laughing as 'DA' and 'BA' flip back and forth; John MacDonald logs 98% adult fusion in the 1976 Nature data sheet. Don't touch the volume knob — just cover the TV screen with a piece of cardboard and watch 'DA-DA' turn instantly into 'BA-BA' (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **3-Year-Olds vs Adults: Growing Stronger Visual Lip Weighting** (*1976, University of Surrey child lab*). The setup centers on a bright play-testing table where a 4-year-old and an adult listen to the same dubbed clip. The child & adult are comparing 59% child fusion vs 98% adult fusion as lip-reading experience matures. Central to the procedure is **age-curve placard ('Age 3-4: 59% to Adult: 98%')** (shows lifelong speech watching strengthens audiovisual coupling; McGurk & MacDonald 1976 developmental data). MacDonald tests child vs adult cohort across identical dub clips. Immediately afterward, the researchers plot developmental rise in visual speech influence. Even four-year-olds fuse sight and sound — and by adulthood 98% of us let lips rewrite our ears (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **2010 TMS Proof: Switching Off the pSTS Mixing Board** (*2010, Human cognitive neuroscience TMS suite*). This phase takes place in a clean bright neurophysiology room where a figure-eight TMS coil rests gently over the left posterior temporal scalp. The neuroscientist is delivering a single harmless magnetic pulse over left pSTS right as the 'BA+GA' clip plays; the volunteer is suddenly reporting the true acoustic 'BA' during pSTS disruption while seeing and hearing normally. Central to the procedure is **handheld figure-eight TMS coil & pSTS target diagram** (temporarily uncouples visual lips from auditory speech; Beauchamp, Nath & Pasalar 2010 causal proof). The neuroscientist triggers brief pSTS pulse, and then volunteer taps 'BA' instead of 'DA'. Across the room, the volunteer is amazed that 'DA' returns the next second without stimulation. Pause one thumbnail-sized patch in the temporal lobe for a tenth of a second, and the phantom 'DA' disappears (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **The Free 15-Decibel Hearing Boost in Noisy Rooms** (*Modern bustling cafe table*). Positioned inside a sunlit lively cafe where two friends turn chairs to face each other directly in clear window light. Two friends are leaning in face-to-face effortlessly catching every word despite background espresso noise. Central to the procedure is **well-lit face-to-face seating** (lets 120-ms visual lip lead prime auditory cortex; everyday communication superpower). The friend a turns toward window light speaking clearly, and then friend B understands effortlessly without shouting. At that moment, both show relaxed, clear conversation. Putting on your glasses in a loud restaurant genuinely helps you HEAR — because your temporal lobe lip-reads every syllable (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Facing away, acoustic accuracy hit 91-99%. Watching the screen, **98% of adults** heard 'ba-voice + ga-lips' fuse into **'da'** (because open-mouth 'ga' rules out sealed-lip 'ba', and tongue-tip 'da' satisfies both senses' best Bayesian compromise!), while 'ga-voice + ba-lips' produced **'bagba/gaba'** combinations (SRC1).

- In 1976 at the University of Surrey, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald were testing how infants respond when a mother's voice comes from a different spot than her face, and asked a video technician to dub the sound **'ba-ba'** onto a close-up film of a woman's lips pronouncing **'ga-ga'**. (SRC1)
- When McGurk and MacDonald played the dubbed video back, neither heard 'ba' OR 'ga' — both swore the loudspeaker was clearly saying **'DA-DA'**. They scolded the technician for mixing up the audio until they closed their eyes — and the exact same speaker instantly said **'BA-BA'**. (SRC1)
- Testing 103 participants across three age brackets (3-to-4-year-olds, 7-to-8-year-olds, and 18-to-40-year-old adults, Nature 1976), **98% of adults** (and 59-81% of young children) reported hearing the fused phantom third syllable **'da'** when watching 'ga' lips over 'ba' sound. (SRC1)
- Even more bizarre was the reverse pairing: dubbing **'ga-ga' audio** onto **'ba-ba' lip closure** didn't fuse into a middle consonant — majorities heard the double-consonant combination **'bagba'** or **'gaba'** because visibly sealed lips ('b') cannot logically produce a back-throat 'g' alone. (SRC1)
- Knowing the trick gives ZERO immunity: Harry McGurk himself knew every frame of the dub, yet opening his eyes flipped his conscious hearing from 'ba' to 'da' every single time for the rest of his life. (SRC1)
- Remarkably, visual speech starts entering the brain ~100-150 milliseconds BEFORE sound hits the cochlea (because lip shaping precedes vocal-cord vibration), letting visual cortex prime auditory cortex and posterior temporal lobe in advance. (SRC2)
- In 2010, Michael Beauchamp, Audrey Nath, and colleagues delivered single-pulse Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) strictly over left posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus (pSTS) right during the syllable: temporarily silencing pSTS abolished the McGurk illusion so subjects heard the true acoustic 'ba' again — without hurting normal single-sense hearing or vision. (SRC2)
- Modern replications (Tiippana, Alsius, Van Engen, SRC3) revealed fascinating individual differences: some healthy adults fuse 95% of trials while others fuse <20% depending on pSTS connectivity, native language phonology (e.g., quieter face-gaze cultures like Japanese show weaker baseline McGurk unless background noise rises), and noisy-room reliance. (SRC3)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Close your eyes and the loudspeaker says **'BA — BA — BA'** with 100% clarity; open your eyes to watch lips mouthing **'GA — GA — GA'**, and your own ears swear the speaker switched to **'DA — DA — DA'** — 98% of adults in McGurk's 1976 paper got fooled — a striking result given that you don't hear with your ears alone; you hear with your eyes (SRC1). Even the two scientists who DISCOVERED the illusion thought their studio technician had botched the audio dub until they covered the TV screen with their hands — notable because conscious knowledge cannot override automatic pSTS sensory fusion (SRC1). Pause the posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus for 100 milliseconds with harmless magnetic stimulation (Beauchamp 2010), and the phantom 'DA' vanishes instantly — restoring raw 'BA' while leaving eyesight and hearing 100% sharp — revealing how causal proof of the exact cortical weld point between sight and sound (SRC2). Because lips visibly move ~120 milliseconds BEFORE vocal cords buzz, watching a speaker's face in a noisy restaurant acts like a free 15-decibel hearing-aid boost — highlighting that everyday speech comprehension is secretly audiovisual (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Why can't even Harry McGurk will the illusion away? Because lip movements start ~120 milliseconds BEFORE vocal cords vibrate, giving visual cortex a head start into the **posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus (pSTS)**. In 2010, Beauchamp, Nath, and Pasalar (SRC2) proved pSTS is the causal weld point: a single harmless TMS pulse over left pSTS broke the weld for 100 milliseconds so volunteers heard the true 'ba' again. Meanwhile 40th-anniversary replications (SRC3) showed intriguing cultural and individual tuning — people rely even more heavily on pSTS lip fusion the moment room noise rises.

- **"Hearing happens strictly along a sealed pipe from ear cochlea to auditory cortex."** — Visual lip geometry reaches posterior temporal and even early auditory cortex milliseconds before sound finishes arriving, co-authoring what you consciously 'hear'. (SRC1)
- **"Once someone explains an illusion, your brain stops falling for it."** — Just like Muller-Lyer lines in vision, McGurk fusion runs below conscious belief — open your eyes, 'da'; close your eyes, 'ba'. (SRC1)
- **"Every human being experiences 100% McGurk fusion on every clip regardless of speaker or culture."** — Modern massive replications show wide healthy person-to-person variation in pSTS audiovisual weighting and stronger visual pull whenever acoustic noise rises. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

The McGurk Effect revolutionized multisensory neuroscience, cinema ADR dubbing, video-call latency standards (why >150 ms lip-sync lag drives viewers crazy!), and speech rehabilitation.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC2** (Audrey R. Nath, Michael S. Beauchamp (2012). A neural basis for interindividual differences in the McGurk effect, a multisensory speech illusion. NeuroImage) provided a fMRI demonstration that left superior temporal sulcus (STS) response amplitude predicts individual susceptibility to the McGurk fusion percept. **SRC3** (Agnès Alsius, Jordi Navarra, Ruth Campbell, Salvador Soto-Faraco (2005). Audiovisual Integration of Speech Falters under High Attention Demands. Current Biology) presented Alsius, Navarra, Campbell & Soto-Faraco (2005 Current Biology) dual-task study showing high visual/auditory attentional load sharply reduces McGurk fusion. **SRC4** (Kaisa Tiippana (2014). What is the McGurk effect? Frontiers in Psychology) contributed a methodological review clarifying stimulus variability and inter-individual boundaries of the McGurk effect. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Ilmari Toivo Johannes Moisio, Tarja Peromaa, Lari Vainio, Martti Vainio et al. (2026). Do acoustic cues influence the McGurk effect—an audiovisual speech illusion? The Journal of the Acoustical Society of America (**SRC5**); and David Meijer, Uta Noppeney (2023). Metacognition in the audiovisual McGurk illusion: perceptual and causal confidence. Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B (**SRC6**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **cochlea** (pure 'BA' acoustic signal) contributes by transmitting an unambiguous bilabial 'BA-BA' sound wave from the loudspeaker on every single repetition, which proves the physical ear input never changes; meanwhile, the **temporal lobe** (pSTS sight-plus-sound fusion hub) contributes by merging visual 'GA' open-lip motion from visual cortex with 'BA' audio from auditory cortex into the compromise percept 'DA', which matches Beauchamp et al. (2010) causal TMS localization in posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus; meanwhile, the **auditory cortex** (rewritten conscious phoneme) contributes by delivering a crisp conscious experience of 'DA-DA' with eyes open that flips instantly back to 'BA-BA' the millisecond eyelids close, which explains why subjects swear the loudspeaker itself changed.

## 8. What it means for you

Discovered by accident in a 1976 British dubbing room, the McGurk Effect proved that 98% of adults hear a brand-new third sound ('DA') when 'BA' audio meets 'GA' lip movements — because the temporal lobe fuses sight and sound before conscious hearing even forms. In noisy restaurants, classrooms, or critical medical conversations, always face your listener in clear light — letting their visual system read lip timing gives an effortless clarity boost equivalent to cranking the volume up.

Never underestimate 'seeing voices': in noisy cafes, classrooms, or family talks with older relatives, face each other in good light — your eyes do half the listening.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **McGurk Effect**: multisensory speech illusion where hearing 'ba' while seeing lips mouth 'ga' makes you consciously hear a fused third syllable ('da' / 'tha') — comparable to your brain averaging ear-input + eye-input into one best-guess compromise.
- **multisensory integration**: combining sight, sound, and touch inside shared brain hubs into one unified perception — comparable to two witnesses comparing notes before filing one joint police report.
- **posterior Superior Temporal Sulcus (pSTS)**: temporal-lobe crossroads where visual lip motion and acoustic phonemes merge — comparable to the mixing board where TMS temporarily switches off the McGurk illusion.
- **visual speech lead (~120 ms)**: the fact that lips visibly shape a consonant over a tenth of a second before sound waves leave the throat — comparable to a visual sneak preview priming auditory cortex.
- **open-eyes / closed-eyes flip**: instant switch between hearing 'da' (eyes open) and 'ba' (eyes shut) on the exact same repeating audio loop — comparable to the fastest self-test in perceptual neuroscience.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Turning on subtitles or putting on your glasses at a loud party and swearing 'Wait — suddenly I can HEAR people better!'.
- Feeling strangely exhausted trying to catch words on an audio-only conference call with choppy microphones vs clear video.
- Opening and closing your eyes during a 5-second 'BA/GA — DA' clip and gasping as the consonant flips mid-sentence.
