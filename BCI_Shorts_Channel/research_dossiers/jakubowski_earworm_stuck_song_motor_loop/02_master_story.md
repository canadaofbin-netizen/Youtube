# Why a 10-Second Song Loop Gets Stuck in Your Head — And How Gum Stops It — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

You walk past a grocery-store speaker playing eight seconds of an upbeat 1980s chorus. Four hours later — at your desk, in traffic, or turning off the bedroom light — that **exact 10-second loop** is still blasting inside your head on endless repeat. Most adults assume an "earworm" lives purely inside the brain's hearing center, and try to crush it by telling themselves *"Stop singing that!"* — which only cranks the volume higher. Yet in **2015**, cognitive psychologist **C. Philip Beaman** and colleagues at the **University of Reading** proved something wonderfully strange across three controlled experiments (`SRC1`): stuck songs survive because **your speech and jaw planner silently mouths every beat**. When 44 volunteers chewed a stick of plain unflavored gum after hearing a catchy pop hook, unwanted earworm replays **dropped by 33 percent** compared with **0 percent** drop sitting still — and in Experiment 3, **chewing gum beat finger-tapping hands down** (`SRC1`).

Staged inside a sunlit Reading psychology lab (`S1`), a 46-year-old volunteer pulls off studio headphones, unwraps a silver foil stick of gum, and chews steadily while computer keypress counts plummet (`SRC1`, `SRC_M1`). Your mental jukebox has a mechanical jaw switch.

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Over **90%** of adults experience involuntary musical imagery (*earworms*) at least once a week (`SRC3`, `SRC6`), yet for decades neuroscience treated stuck tunes as mysterious, uncontrollable echoes in auditory memory. Worse, under Daniel Wegner's thought-suppression rules, adding a "distracting task" while trying to suppress an unwanted thought usually makes the thought rebound *harder* — unless the task physically occupies the exact circuit generating the loop!

Between **2013 and 2022**, a wave of rigorous music-cognition and MRI studies — led by **Ira Hyman (2013, `SRC4`)**, **Nicolas Farrugia, Kelly Jakubowski, and Lauren Stewart at Goldsmiths, University of London (2015 & 2017, `SRC3`, `SRC2`)**, **Philip Beaman at Reading (2015, `SRC1`)**, and **Benjamin Kubit & Petr Janata at UC Davis (2022, `SRC5`)** — mapped the exact melodic, structural, and motor blueprint of earworms.

## 3. People

- **C. Philip Beaman, Kitty Powell & Ellie Rapley (University of Reading, 2015)**: Experimental psychologists who tested whether sub-vocal articulatory interference via plain chewing gum blocks both voluntary musical imagery and involuntary earworms (`SRC1`).
- **Kelly Jakubowski, Nicolas Farrugia, Rhodri Cusack, Lauren Stewart & Daniel Müllensiefen (Goldsmiths London, 2015–2017)**: Cognitive neuroscientists who mapped cortical thickness in frequent earworm sufferers (`SRC3`) and dissected **3,000** listener survey reports across **100 earworm hits vs 100 non-earworm chart controls** (`SRC2`).
- **Ira E. Hyman Jr. et al. (2013) & Benjamin M. Kubit and Petr Janata (2022)**: Applied memory scientists proving familiar, well-liked songs hijack low-load autopilot routines (`SRC4`) while secretly helping consolidate real-world episodic memories (`SRC5`).

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Beaman's three-experiment 2015 study (`SRC1`) and Jakubowski's 3,000-person structural analysis (`SRC2`) revealed both *how* a song hooks your motor cortex and *how* to unhook it:

1. **2015 University of Reading Lab: Chewing Gum Beats Finger-Tapping at Silencing Keypress Earworms (`S1`, Iconic)**. In **Experiment 1**, **44 participants** listened to the infectious hook of David Guetta's *"Play Hard"*, then completed two **3-minute** tracking blocks (trying to suppress the song first, then thinking freely) while pressing a computer key every time the tune popped into awareness (`SRC1`). Chewing plain gum slashed earworm keypresses by **one-third (~33%)** across both blocks! In **Experiment 2**, gum specifically reduced *hearing* the vivid musical audio rather than vague thoughts *about* the song. And in **Experiment 3**, Beaman pitted **gum chewing** directly against **rhythmic finger-tapping** — proving jaw articulation cuts earworms significantly more than hand motor distraction (`SRC1`, `SRC_M1`, `SRC_M2`).
2. **Outdoor Morning Sidewalk Walk: How 124-BPM Songs Lock Onto Human Walking Tempo (`S2`, Outdoor)**. Out on a sunlit morning sidewalk outside a corner bakery, a 50-year-old commuter catches a radio chorus and strides down the block in lockstep. Why did *that* song stick? Comparing **100 top earworms against 100 matched chart songs** that never got stuck across **3,000 people**, **Jakubowski et al. (2017, `SRC2`)** found earworms cluster around **~124 beats per minute** (matching natural human walking tempo!) and combine an easy rising-and-falling pitch arch with one distinctive interval leap.
3. **Evening Kitchen Dish-Washing Loop & Spoken Crossword Reset (`S3`)**. At the kitchen sink after dinner, rinsing plates on low-load autopilot (`SRC4`) lets the auditory-motor loop spin up again — until popping a piece of mint gum or speaking three crossword clues aloud engages **Broca's speech center** and clears the mental deck before sleep (`SRC1`, `SRC4`).

Visual staging and pacing were cross-checked against analyzed keyframes in BrainCraft's `"Why Do Songs Get Stuck In Your Head?"` (`SRC_M1`, 12 extracted frames in `media/M1/`) and `"Interesting Facts: How to Get Rid of Songs Stuck in Your Head"` (`SRC_M2`).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

- **33% Reduction via Chewing Gum (`SRC1`)**: Across **44 volunteers** in Experiment 1 (`3-minute` suppression and expression windows), chewing gum reduced conscious earworm episodes by roughly **one-third (33%)** compared with **0%** reduction in no-gum controls — and significantly outperformed finger-tapping in Experiment 3 (`SRC1`).
- **3,000 Listeners & 124 BPM Motor Sweet Spot (`SRC2`)**: Analyzing **3,000** survey respondents and **100 earworm tracks vs 100 matched chart non-earworms**, Jakubowski et al. (2017) isolated faster average tempo (**~124 bpm**), generic arch contours, and unusual interval leaps as the statistical signature of sticky music.
- **Cortical Thickness Signature (`SRC3`)**: Voxel-based cortical morphometry by **Farrugia, Jakubowski, Cusack, and Stewart (2015)** linked earworm frequency and emotional reaction to cortical structure in **right Heschl's gyrus (auditory cortex)** and **right inferior frontal gyrus**, confirming an anatomical **auditory–motor–frontal** loop.
- **24-Hour Recurrence & Memory Consolidation (`SRC4`, `SRC5`)**: **Hyman et al. (2013, `SRC4`)** showed songs that replay right after listening are far more likely to return intrusively within **24 hours**, especially during mind-wandering chores; **Kubit and Janata (2022, `SRC5`)** proved spontaneous mental replay strengthens memory for everyday scenes paired with that music!

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

1. **You Don't Catch Songs You Hate — You Catch Songs You Like**: Pop folklore blames hated commercial jingles, yet **Hyman et al. (2013, `SRC4`)** and **Cotter et al. (2016, `SRC6`)** proved familiar songs people actively enjoy account for the overwhelming majority of everyday earworms.
2. **An Earworm Is a Silent Duet Between Ear and Jaw**: Pure auditory echo fades in seconds. A song stays stuck for hours only when **auditory cortex** hands the baton to **premotor cortex** and **Broca's speech/jaw planner** (`SRC1`, `SRC3`), turning your own silent vocal tract into a repeat button.
3. **Hidden Memory Helper**: **Kubit and Janata (2022, `SRC5`)** discovered that involuntary song replay acts like sleep replay — tagging and strengthening incidental memories from earlier in your day!

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Together, Beaman (2015), Farrugia & Jakubowski (2015–2017), and Kubit & Janata (2022) reframed earworms from a quirky nuisance into a window on **auditory-motor working memory** and **hippocampal-cortical replay**.

Inside the 3D brain (`06_brain_link.json`), three canonical regions run the loop:
- **Auditory cortex (`sound center`, `active`)**: Reconstructs vivid melody and timbre above the ears (`SRC3`).
- **Premotor cortex (`action planner`, `active`)**: Locks onto ~124-bpm movement rhythm and cues the next bar (`SRC2`).
- **Broca's area (`speech center`, `silenced` by gum)**: Silently mouths the hook in the inferior frontal gyrus until rhythmic gum chewing occupies jaw-motor planning and breaks the loop (`SRC1`, `SRC3`).

## 8. What it means for you

When a 10-second chorus hijacks your focus during work or right before bed:

- **Chew Plain Gum for 2–3 Minutes**: Occupy your sub-vocal jaw planner directly (`SRC1`) — finger-tapping or foot-shaking won't touch it.
- **No Gum? Speak Out Loud**: Read a paragraph, recite tomorrow's grocery list, or work a quick verbal anagram (`SRC4`) to commandeer Broca's area without straining.
- **Appreciate the Replay**: If a nostalgic song pops up gently on a walk, let it finish once — your brain may be filing good memories (`SRC5`).

Plain-language takeaways:
- **Involuntary musical imagery**: A 10-second song hook repeating without your permission.
- **Articulatory motor loop**: Your silent speech and jaw planner mouthing the tune backstage.
- **124-bpm motor entrainment**: Upbeat tempos locking onto human walking speed.
- **Phonological interference**: Keeping your jaw busy so the silent chorus drops the line.
