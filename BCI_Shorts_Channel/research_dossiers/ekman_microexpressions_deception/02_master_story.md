# Paul Ekman & Wallace Friesen (1969-1978): Frame-by-Frame Microexpressions, FACS & the 'Pinocchio Nose' Myth — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 1969 in San Francisco, a patient named Mary smiled brightly through a filmed discharge interview — then confessed days later she had been hiding overwhelming despair the entire time (SRC1). Baffled that experienced doctors saw nothing, Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen cranked the 16-millimeter film back and stepped it forward one frame at a time: right at Frame 14, for barely a twelfth of a second, acute anguish flashed across her brow before her polite smile clamped it shut.

Recorded in 1969, Langley Porter Neuropsychiatric Institute film lab, UC San Francisco, the defining visual record centers on two 1969 psychologists at a 16mm flatbed film-editing console pointing at a frozen two-frame facial expression strip beside an anatomical muscle Action Unit chart (SRC1, SRC_STILL). By stepping 16mm interview film one frame at a time in 1969, Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen discovered split-second 'microexpressions' where subcortical emotion leaks out before a polite mask snaps shut — while proving that reading emotion is NEVER a magic lie detector (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Anthropologists like Margaret Mead argued facial expressions were arbitrary cultural conventions learned like spoken alphabets, while clinicians relied on unverified intuition to judge sincerity.

At Langley Porter Neuropsychiatric Institute, UC San Francisco in 1969, **Paul Ekman & Wallace Friesen's slow-motion clinical film leakage discovery & Facial Action Coding System (FACS)** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Paul Ekman, Wallace V. Friesen (1969). Nonverbal Leakage and Clues to Deception †. Psychiatry).

## 3. People

- **Paul Ekman**: UCSF psychologist who discovered brief involuntary microexpressions on slow-motion interview film while also warning that no facial tic guarantees lying (SRC1).
- **Wallace V. Friesen**: Collaborator who co-discovered clinical microexpressions (1969) and co-developed the 44-Action-Unit Facial Action Coding System growing out of their 1969 nonverbal-leakage framework (SRC1).
- **Ernest Haggard & Kenneth Isaacs**: Researchers who first spotted 'micromomentary expressions' (1966) during slow-motion psychotherapy film review (SRC1).

Paul Ekman (b. 1934) and Wallace V. Friesen extended Haggard & Isaacs's 1966 film observations into cross-cultural emotion research and the 1978 Facial Action Coding System (FACS); Stephen Porter, Maria Hartwig, and Charles Bond later established strict empirical bounds on lie detection.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Ekman and Friesen analyzed high-stakes deceptive vs truthful clinical interviews frame by frame at 24 fps (SRC1) and subsequently spent eight years mapping all 44 independent facial muscle Action Units (FACS, SRC2).

1. **Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen advancing a 16mm Steenbeck film viewer one frame at a time to catch a 2-frame brow flash** (*1969, Langley Porter Neuropsychiatric Institute film lab, UC San Francisco*). The scene unfolds inside a sunlit 1969 UCSF film-analysis room featuring a flatbed 16mm Steenbeck editing console with glowing ground-glass viewing screen, numbered frame counter ('Frame 14 to Frame 15'), anatomical Action Unit charts pinned on the wall, and clipboards. Paul Ekman (wearing 1969 tweed blazer and open shirt) is turning the hand-crank frame advance knob one click at a time while pointing a grease pencil at Frame 14 on the bright viewer screen; Wallace Friesen (wearing 1969 cardigan and glasses) is holding a FACS muscle-anatomy chart beside the screen logging Action Units AU1+4 before AU12 snaps on at Frame 17. The experimental setup features **16mm flatbed Steenbeck film viewer** (brushed-aluminum editing table displaying crisp sequential portrait frames on its frosted screen; freezes the 83-millisecond brow flash invisible at 24 fps), **three-frame contact strip ('Frame 13 Neutral / Frame 14-15 Distress / Frame 17 Polite Smile')** (enlarged black-and-white photographic strip resting on the desk; makes the two-frame leak unmistakable), and **anatomical Facial Action Coding chart** (clean medical line drawing mapping numbered forehead and cheek muscles (AU1, AU4, AU6, AU12); anchors objective muscle coding). Paul Ekman plays the 16mm interview at full speed showing an unbroken polite smile, then rewinds, and finally clicks the hand wheel two single frames forward exposing the hidden distress brow. Wallace Friesen matches the brow angle to Action Units 1+4 on the anatomical chart. In response, Paul Ekman circles 'Frame 14-15 (0.08 sec)' on the strip in astonishment; Wallace Friesen writes 'Concealed Distress — Check Context (Avoid Othello's Error)' in the margin. Invisible at full 24-frames-per-second speed, stepping the 16mm film two frames forward exposes an 83-millisecond flash of anguish before the polite smile locks shut (SRC1, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **Eight years mapping 44 Facial Action Units in a mirror** (*1976, UCSF facial anatomy lab*). The setup centers on a bright studio desk with an angled mirror, anatomical skull-muscle drawings, and numbered AU reference cards. Ekman & Friesen are isolating single brow and cheek contractions in a desk mirror to verify Action Unit independence. Central to the procedure is **44 Action Unit reference binder** (illustrated muscle atlas; replaces vague adjectives with exact muscle mechanics). Wallace Friesen isolates AU6 cheek-raiser + AU12 lip-puller vs AU12 alone. Immediately afterward, Paul Ekman photographs the crisp anatomical distinction. Cataloging 44 exact muscle units turned fuzzy 'intuition' into repeatable science — and later Pixar animation (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **Othello's Error: Innocent anxiety vs guilty anxiety look identical** (*Modern interview room*). The setup centers on split comparison of two identical interview tables where an honest nervous witness and a deceptive witness show the exact same tense brow. The interviewer is pausing judgment and checking verifiable timeline documents instead of guessing from nerves. Central to the procedure is **objective timeline verification folder** (hard factual evidence vs 54% demeanor guessing; prevents false accusations). The interviewer sets aside gut hunches, and then asks calm open-ended timeline questions. Across the room, the honest nervous witness relaxes once treated fairly. Fear on an innocent face looks identical to fear on a guilty face — never confuse emotion with a lie (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **Using a fleeting expression as an empathy cue, not a gotcha** (*Modern coffee chat*). Positioned inside a sunlit office lounge where one colleague notices a brief flash of concern when deadlines come up and gently asks 'Do we need more runway?'. Two colleague are seated across a small round table in open supportive conversation. Central to the procedure is **project calendar pad** (adjusted collaboratively on the table; solves the hidden worry constructively). The colleague a spots brief tension behind 'Sure, fine!', then gently asks 'What part feels tight?', and finally colleague B opens up relieved. At that moment, the colleague B exhales gratefully and points out the bottleneck. Used with humility, noticing a fleeting flash opens compassionate conversation instead of armchair policing (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

When strong emotion clashes with deliberate suppression, subcortical limbic pathways fire facial muscles tens of milliseconds before voluntary motor cortex masks them, leaking brief (40-200 ms) microexpressions or partial upper/lower-face mismatches (SRC1, SRC2).

- In 1969 at UC San Francisco, Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen analyzed 16mm clinical interview films frame by frame at 24 frames per second after a hospitalized patient ('Mary') later confessed she had been hiding acute despair behind a cheerful smile to win a weekend pass. (SRC1)
- Played at normal speed, Mary looked convincingly upbeat; stepped frame by frame, a two-frame (1/12-second) flash of intense anguish broke across her brow and mouth before a polite social smile snapped over it. (SRC1)
- Building on Haggard & Isaacs (1966), Ekman and Friesen termed these involuntary flashes 'microexpressions' — brief emotional leaks lasting roughly 40 to 200 milliseconds (1/25 to 1/5 of a second) when someone tries to conceal strong affect. (SRC1)
- To measure faces objectively without guesswork, Ekman and Friesen spent eight years dissecting facial anatomy to build the Facial Action Coding System (FACS, 1976-1978), cataloging 44 distinct muscular Action Units (e.g., AU6 orbicularis oculi cheek-raiser + AU12 lip-corner puller in a genuine Duchenne smile). (SRC2)
- Dual motor wiring explains the leak: voluntary polite smiles travel from motor cortex, whereas urgent emotional expressions fire automatically from subcortical limbic/amygdala-brainstem nuclei milliseconds before cortical suppression catches up. (SRC2)
- Crucially, Ekman himself coined 'Othello's Error': spotting a 100-millisecond flash of fear on an innocent suspect's face does NOT prove lying — an honest person terrified of being disbelieved flashes the exact same fear as a guilty liar terrified of getting caught. (SRC1)
- Empirical high-stakes courtroom and laboratory studies by Stephen Porter & Leanne ten Brinke (2008) confirmed complete full-face microexpressions are actually rare (appearing in only ~2% of expressions, mostly partial upper- or lower-face leaks) and untrained human lie-detection accuracy averages just 54 percent — barely above a 50/50 toss. (SRC4)
- Cross-cultural field studies with isolated Fore communities in Papua New Guinea (Ekman & Friesen, 1971) showed shared recognition of core macro-expressions (anger, fear, disgust, sadness, enjoyment) while cultural 'display rules' govern when people mask them in public. (SRC3)
- Slow-motion 24-fps film inspection (isolating 1-to-2-frame leaks around frame 14 on a tabletop Steenbeck viewer) inspired Ekman's microexpression model, though Porter and ten Brinke (2008) found full-face leaks (<1/10 to 1/5 second) in only ~2% of trials. (SRC4)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. At normal 24-frames-per-second playback, seasoned psychiatrists saw only a calm smile — stepping the 16mm projector frame by frame exposed a 2-frame (83-millisecond) flash of agony hidden in plain sight — a striking result given that slow-motion film revealed a tug-of-war between two separate facial motor systems (SRC1). Paul Ekman named psychology's deadliest lie-detection trap after Shakespeare's Othello: Othello saw genuine fear on Desdemona's face and murdered her assuming fear meant guilt — when she was terrified of not being believed — notable because a microexpression reads hidden EMOTION, never a Pinocchio lie (SRC1). Across hundreds of studies (Bond & DePaulo 2006), police, judges, and customs officers spot lies from demeanor at roughly 54 percent accuracy — virtually a 50/50 guess — revealing how Pop-TV 'human lie detector' infallibility is pure fiction (SRC4). Ekman and Friesen personally stimulated and photographed every single muscle fiber in their own faces for eight years to map 44 objective Action Units — later adopted by Pixar animators to bring Toy Story and Inside Out faces to life — highlighting that clinical anatomy became the language of modern digital cinema (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Pop TV shows like Lie to Me turned microexpressions into an infallible superpower — a myth both data and Ekman's own 'Othello's Error' warn against. High-stakes lab studies (Porter & ten Brinke 2008; Bond & DePaulo 2006, SRC4) proved full microexpressions occur in only ~2% of trials, human demeanor lie-detection sits near 54% (barely a coin toss), and a flash of fear NEVER tells you whether someone is lying or simply afraid of being wrongly accused.

- **"There is a 'Pinocchio nose' facial tic or eye-direction tell ('looking up-left = lying') that proves deception 100% of the time."** — Zero universal Pinocchio tells exist; eye-gaze direction myths fail completely under controlled testing, and microexpressions show concealed emotion rather than deception. (SRC4)
- **"Liars flash full-face microexpressions every few seconds like TV crime dramas."** — Porter & ten Brinke (2008) showed partial upper- or lower-face leakage is far commoner and full-face microexpressions appear on only ~2% of emotional trials. (SRC4)
- **"You can force a completely convincing 'genuine' eye-crinkling smile on command whenever stressed."** — Most adults struggle to voluntarily contract the outer orbicularis oculi (AU6) symmetrically without genuine affect while suppressing brow distress (AU1+4). (SRC2)
- **"Microexpression science justifies airport behavior-screening arrests without hard evidence."** — Independent scientific audits criticized TSA's SPOT program because nervousness and fatigue mimic 'deception' cues (Othello's Error). (SRC4)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

FACS became the global gold standard for affective neuroscience, depression recovery research, computer vision, and character animation, while policing shifted from 'body-language myths' toward cognitive evidence interviewing.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC4** (Stephen Porter, Leanne ten Brinke (2008). Reading Between the Lies. Psychological Science) contributed an empirical high-speed video test showing complete full-face microexpressions occur in roughly 2% of high-stakes emotional concealment trials. At the neural-circuit level, the **amygdala** (fast emotion trigger) contributes by firing rapid involuntary facial commands through brainstem motor nuclei within tens of milliseconds, which Explains why the 2-frame emotional flash escapes before conscious masking begins; meanwhile, the **motor cortex** (voluntary mask controller) contributes by clamping a deliberate social smile over the lower face a fraction of a second later, which shows the cortical-vs-subcortical race captured on frame-by-frame film.

## 8. What it means for you

By stepping 16mm interview film one frame at a time in 1969, Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen discovered split-second 'microexpressions' where subcortical emotion leaks out before a polite mask snaps shut — while proving that reading emotion is NEVER a magic lie detector. Treat a brief flash of worry or frustration on someone's face as an invitation for empathy, never an accusation of lying — an innocent person afraid of being doubted looks just as tense as a liar.

Burn the 'looking up-left means lying' myth forever. If you catch a split-second shadow cross a friend's face before 'I'm fine,' treat it as a gentle cue to ask how you can help — never a courtroom verdict.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **microexpression**: involuntary facial expression flashing for ~1/25 to 1/5 of a second when someone tries to hide a strong emotion — like steam puffing briefly from under a pot lid before a hand presses it down.
- **nonverbal leakage**: emotional signals escaping through muscles harder to control consciously — like a calm voice paired with white-knuckled fingers.
- **FACS (Facial Action Coding System)**: objective 44-unit anatomical catalog of individual facial muscle contractions — like sheet music notation for every muscle in the human face.
- **Othello's Error**: mistaking an honest person's fear of being disbelieved for a liar's fear of getting caught — like blaming a nervous innocent traveler at customs.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Catching a split-second tighten around a friend's eyes right before they smile and say 'No, really, it's totally fine!'.
- Stopping yourself from accusing a nervous teenager or job candidate of lying just because their voice trembles under authority ('Othello's Error').
- Spotting a polite mouth-only 'Pan-Am smile' (AU12 alone) vs a warm eye-crinkling Duchenne smile (AU6 + AU12).
- Asking a gentle open question ('Did something about that timeline worry you?') instead of playing amateur mind-reader.
