# Watson & Rayner (1920): 'Little Albert', the Claw-Hammer Steel Bar & History's Most Controversial Fear Test — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

On a flickering 1920 silent film reel shot at Johns Hopkins Hospital, a calm nine-month-old baby boy sits on a white mattress and reaches out with zero fear to pat a live white rat, a rabbit, and a dog. Two months later on the exact same reel, John B. Watson stands out of sight behind the baby's head holding a carpenter's claw hammer beside a four-foot steel bar (SRC1). Seven metallic clangs later, the silent white rat alone makes the baby recoil — launching the most famous, most misquoted, and most ethically condemned experiment in early behaviorism.

Recorded in 1920, Phipps Clinic nursery room, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, the defining visual record centers on a 1920 white-sheeted nursery testing table with an infant figure, a rat and wooden blocks in front, and a researcher standing behind with a suspended 4-foot steel bar and hammer (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 1920, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner showed that seven pairings of an unexpected steel-bar clang with a tame white rat taught 11-month-old 'Little Albert' to fear furry cues — a famous demonstration of emotional conditioning clouded by fading effects, textbook myths, and never reversing the child's fear (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Freudian psychoanalysts interpreted childhood animal phobias as intricate unconscious family symbols, while Ivan Pavlov had thus far conditioned digestive reflexes in dogs rather than human emotions.

At Harriet Lane Home / Phipps Psychiatric Clinic nursery room, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore in 1920, **John B. Watson & Rosalie Rayner's 1920 Johns Hopkins 'Little Albert' Conditioned Emotional Reactions study** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: John B. Watson, Rosalie Rayner (1920). Conditioned emotional reactions. Journal of Experimental Psychology).

## 3. People

- **John B. Watson & Rosalie Rayner**: Founder of Behaviorism and graduate student who filmed baseline fearlessness at 9 months and paired a white rat with a steel-bar clang at 11 months, 3 days (SRC1).
- **'Albert B.'**: Infant child of a Harriet Lane Home wet nurse — calm and unafraid of animals on film at baseline, removed from the hospital before any fear reversal was ever attempted (SRC1).
- **Ben Harris, Hall P. Beck & Alan J. Fridlund**: Historians of psychology whose archival investigations exposed textbook exaggerations, unreversed conditioning ethics, and the Douglas Merritte vs Albert Barger identity debate (SRC6).

John B. Watson (1878-1958) and graduate student Rosalie Rayner (1898-1935) tested 'Albert B.', infant son of a hospital wet nurse, just months before scandal forced Watson out of academia into Madison Avenue advertising; four years later Watson's student Mary Cover Jones (1897-1987) pioneered the humane cure.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

After filming fearless baseline play at ~9 months, Watson and Rayner (1920, SRC1) waited until 11 months, 3 days, then struck the suspended steel bar right behind Albert whenever his hand touched the white rat (7 pairings across two sessions), followed 5 days and 31 days later by generalization checks with a rabbit, dog, sealskin coat, cotton wool, Santa mask, and neutral wooden control blocks.

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Albert cried and crawled away from the silent rat after 7 pairings and showed varying avoidance of furry items (strongest to the rabbit and dog, weaker to cotton), while playing normally with wooden blocks right between trials (SRC1).

- At 8 months, 26 days old in late 1919 at Johns Hopkins, 'Albert B.' was filmed on a white nursery mattress reaching out calmly with ZERO fear toward a tame white rat, a rabbit, a dog, cotton wool, masks, and even burning newspaper. (SRC1)
- Only ONE stimulus triggered an unconditioned startle reflex in Albert: Watson standing behind the infant's back and striking a suspended 4-foot-long, 3/4-inch steel bar with a carpenter's claw hammer. (SRC1)
- Starting at 11 months, 3 days old (Watson & Rayner, 1920), each time Albert's fingers touched the white rat, Watson slammed the steel bar behind his head — twice in Session 1, then five more pairings a week later (7 total pairings). (SRC1)
- After 7 pairings, presenting the silent white rat ALONE caused Albert to cry, pucker his face, and crawl rapidly away across the mattress — conditioned fear transferred to a previously neutral animal. (SRC1)
- Five days later (11 months, 20 days), Watson and Rayner tested 'stimulus generalization': Albert whimpered or pulled back from a furry rabbit, a dog, a sealskin fur coat, cotton wool, and Watson wearing a white-bearded Santa Claus mask — while continuing to play happily with wooden toy blocks right between trials. (SRC1)
- Crucial historical critique #1 (Ben Harris, 1979; Powell et al., 2014): introductory psychology textbooks blatantly invented claims Watson never saw (like fear of a 'white teddy bear' or 'his mother's fur coat'), hid that Watson repeatedly had to 'freshen' the fading fear with extra hammer strikes, and ignored that Albert frequently sucked his thumb to block fear. (SRC4)
- Crucial ethics failure #2: Watson & Rayner KNEW a month in advance when Albert's mother would take him home, yet chose to spend his final sessions testing generalization rather than extinguishing the conditioned fear — it took Watson's student Mary Cover Jones ('Little Peter', 1924) four years later to pioneer gentle exposure counterconditioning. (SRC1)
- Modern archival sleuthing sparked a lively historical detective story over Albert's real identity — Hall Beck & Alan Fridlund (2009/2012) proposed neurologically ill 'Douglas Merritte' (who died at age 6), whereas Russ Powell, Nancy Digdon & Ben Harris (2014) unearthed strong hospital-census evidence for healthy 'William Albert Barger' (1919-2007), who lived to 87 and reportedly disliked dogs. (SRC6)
- In Watson and Rayner's (1920) transfer tests 5 days after rat-plus-steel-bar conditioning, fear responses generalized to a rabbit, dog, sealskin coat, and Santa Claus mask; in 1924, Mary Cover Jones extinguished fear in child 'Peter' by moving a caged rabbit from 12 feet to 0 feet during snack feeding. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. On Watson's surviving silent 1920 film reel, 9-month-old Albert reaches happily for a rat, a rabbit, and a dog — until 7 clangs of a 4-foot steel bar behind his head turn soft white fur alone into an instant alarm trigger — a striking result given that seven acoustic pairings wired a brand-new emotional reflex before language even existed (SRC1). Right between crying at the rabbit and pulling back from a Santa beard, Albert smiled and banged wooden blocks cleanly — proving wooden blocks acted as a control ensuring he wasn't just crying at everything in the room — notable because even in N=1, wooden blocks separated conditioned fur cues from general distress (SRC1). When historians audited 100+ college textbooks (Harris 1979; Powell 2014), over half got the facts wrong — inventing animals Watson never used and hiding that Albert's conditioned fear faded quickly unless Watson struck the steel bar again — revealing how always read the original 1920 laboratory diary instead of textbook folklore (SRC4). Watson never un-conditioned Little Albert — it was graduate student Mary Cover Jones in 1924 ('Little Peter', pairing snacks with gradually closer rabbits) who invented modern exposure therapy — highlighting that the cure came from Mary Cover Jones, not John Watson (SRC3).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Generations of textbooks turned N=1 Little Albert into an exaggerated fairy tale — inventing 'white teddy bears' Watson never owned, hiding that Albert's conditioned fear faded markedly after a couple of weeks unless 'freshened' with new hammer strikes, and glossing over the gravest ethical failure: Watson & Rayner knew a month ahead when Albert was leaving, yet never extinguished his fear (SRC2, SRC3). Historians even clashed over who Albert was — sickly Douglas Merritte (Beck & Fridlund 2009) vs healthy William Albert Barger, who lived to 87 (Powell, Digdon & Harris 2014).

- **"Little Albert's conditioned rat phobia stayed rock-solid and permanent from the very first session."** — In Watson & Rayner's own 1920 notes, Albert's fear noticeably weakened after 10-30 days, forcing them to strike the steel bar again during generalization sessions to 'freshen' the reaction. (SRC1)
- **"Watson proved every human phobia comes purely from a single loud traumatic pairing."** — Evolutionary preparedness (Seligman/Öhman) later showed humans condition easily to snakes, heights, or animals, whereas conditioning lifelong phobias to wooden blocks, curtains, or spoons almost never sticks. (SRC2)
- **"Watson & Rayner cured Albert before he left Johns Hopkins."** — They never attempted extinction or counterconditioning despite knowing his departure date — an ethical violation permanently banned by modern Institutional Review Boards. (SRC1)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Little Albert became both a cautionary pillar of Institutional Review Board (IRB) child-protection rules and — thanks to Mary Cover Jones's 1924 'Little Peter' counterconditioning study — the springboard for modern graduated exposure therapy.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC4** (Ben Harris (1979). Whatever happened to little Albert? American Psychologist) contributed Ben Harris's critical archival audit exposing textbook distortions of Watson and Rayner's 1920 single-infant conditioning report. **SRC5** (Mary Cover Jones (1991). A Laboratory Study of Fear: The Case of Peter. The Journal of Genetic Psychology) offered Mary Cover Jones's 1924 counterconditioning reversal of childhood animal phobia via graded distance and food pairing. **SRC6** (Hall P. Beck, Sharman Levinson, Gary Irons (2009). Finding little Albert: A journey to John B. Watson’s infant laboratory. American Psychologist) provided a historical archival investigation into the identity and clinical record of the infant filmed by Watson and Rayner at Johns Hopkins. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Russell A. Powell, Nancy Digdon, Ben Harris, Christopher Smithson (2014). Little Albert’s alleged neurological impairment: Watson, Rayner, and historical revision. History of Psychology (**SRC3**).

## 8. What it means for you

In 1920, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner showed that seven pairings of an unexpected steel-bar clang with a tame white rat taught 11-month-old 'Little Albert' to fear furry cues — a famous demonstration of emotional conditioning clouded by fading effects, textbook myths, and never reversing the child's fear. When an everyday place, sound, or animal triggers an outsized gut flinch from one bad past pairing, remember Mary Cover Jones's 1924 antidote: calm, gradual, safe re-exposure teaches the alarm system that the cue no longer predicts danger.

Conditioned fear is NOT a permanent life sentence: what seven pairings wired in fades naturally when no longer reinforced and un-wires steadily through safe, calm, graduated exposure.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **conditioned emotional reaction**: when a neutral sight or touch (white rat) triggers genuine fear after being paired with an innate startle shock (steel-bar clang) — comparable to flinching at a ringtone that used to bring emergency work calls.
- **stimulus generalization**: when a learned fear spreads from the original trigger (white rat) to resembling cues (white rabbit, fur coat, white beard) — comparable to getting burned by one hot cast-iron pan and hesitating around black skillets.
- **control toy blocks**: neutral wooden blocks Albert played with happily between fur tests, proving he wasn't simply crying at the whole room — comparable to the baseline check inside Watson's 1920 sessions.
- **counterconditioning (Mary Cover Jones 1924)**: gradually pairing a feared object at a safe distance with calm comfort until fear dissolves — comparable to the humane antidote Watson failed to give Little Albert.
- **preparedness**: why animal triggers condition far more easily than curtains or wooden blocks — comparable to ancient survival wiring favoring biological cues.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Flinching instantly at the smell of a dentist's antiseptic or a hospital corridor sound because of one painful childhood visit.
- Understanding why gentle, graduated exposure ('Little Peter' style) steadily un-wires conditioned anxiety.
- Checking primary sources whenever a famous textbook story sounds suspiciously neat.
