# The Invisible Gorilla — Selective Attention and Inattentional Blindness (Simons & Chabris, 1999) — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)
On the fourteenth floor of William James Hall at Harvard University in 1999, six undergraduate students gather in a narrow, windowless elevator lobby. Three wear plain white t-shirts; three wear black t-shirts. Behind them stand three brushed-steel double elevator doors along a pale cream wall marked with taped black 'S' target curves. For just over a minute, the six students weave in tight figure-eights across the grey-beige floor, bouncing and tossing two bright orange basketballs back and forth. Viewers watching the tape are given a single, simple instruction: silently count how many times the players in white pass the ball. Thirty-five seconds into the clip, a seventh figure—Harvard undergraduate Elisa Cheng dressed head-to-toe in a shaggy, dark brown-black gorilla costume—strolls casually into the frame from the right side. She walks right between the weaving players, stops dead center in front of the middle elevator door, turns to face the camera squarely, thumps her chest with both furry fists, and walks calmly out the left side. She is on screen for nine full seconds. Yet when the video stops after fifteen passes and the experimenter asks, 'Did you see the gorilla?', half of all viewers stare back in disbelief and insist there was no gorilla.

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)
For nearly a century, psychologists and everyday observers alike assumed that human vision worked like a high-resolution movie camera: if your eyes are open, healthy, and pointed at a well-lit scene, anything large and unusual moving across the center of your gaze will automatically grab your awareness. In 1907, Hungarian neurologist Rezső Bálint had noted clinically that extreme absorption in one object could leave a person blind to surrounding objects even when light rays reached the visual cortex intact. In the late 1970s, Cornell cognitive psychologist Ulric Neisser and his colleagues Robert Becklen and Evelyn Dube tested 'selective looking' by using angled half-silvered mirrors to superimpose two separate videotapes—a basketball-passing game and a hand-slapping game—and found that viewers pressing a button for each pass rarely noticed when a woman carrying an open white umbrella walked through the ghostly, semi-transparent frame for four seconds. In 1998, psychologists Arien Mack and Irvin Rock coined the term 'inattentional blindness' using brief 200-millisecond computer flashes of crosses and small geometric shapes. Yet mainstream vision scientists remained skeptical: surely people only missed Mack and Rock's shapes because they flashed for a fifth of a second, and surely people only missed Neisser's umbrella woman because the superimposed video looked ghostly and artificial. Nobody believed that a healthy observer would miss a solid, full-sized, bizarre intruder in a live-action scene for five to nine seconds.

## 3. People
In 1998 and 1999, Daniel J. Simons, a newly appointed assistant professor of psychology at Harvard University, teamed up with Christopher F. Chabris, a Harvard psychology PhD student teaching an undergraduate research methods laboratory course. Determined to test whether sustained inattentional blindness happened in fully opaque, live-action scenes, they borrowed a full-body gorilla costume from renowned Harvard developmental psychologist Jerome 'Jerry' Kagan (who used the suit in toddler temperament experiments down the hall). With the corridor outside Kagan's lab empty between classes, Simons set up a Panasonic AG456U SVHS camera on the fourteenth-floor elevator landing of William James Hall while Chabris choreographed the actors so they could weave and pass two basketballs without colliding. Three researchers formed the White team (Judith Danovitch, Steve Most, and Alex Wong), three formed the Black team (Amy DeIpolyi, Jason Jay, and Megan White), Jennifer Shephard played the tall Umbrella Woman, and undergraduate Elisa Cheng climbed inside Kagan's hot, furry gorilla suit. Twenty-one undergraduate student experimenters then took VHS copies of the edited tapes across campus to test 228 individual volunteers on 13-to-36-inch television monitors.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step
Simons and Chabris constructed a rigorous 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design across sixteen experimental conditions, plus a separate live-action chest-thumping gorilla demonstration. First, they filmed four 75-second main videotapes in the 3-meter-deep by 5.2-meter-wide elevator bay: two Transparent tapes (where the White team, Black team, and unexpected walker were filmed separately and digitally superimposed with Adobe After Effects on a Media 100LX system) and two Opaque live-action tapes (where all seven actors performed together in a single take, physically occluding one another and the basketballs). After 44 to 48 seconds of passing, either the Umbrella Woman walked from left to right for 5 seconds, or the Gorilla walked through the action for 5 seconds. Second, each volunteer was assigned to attend to either the White-shirt team or the Black-shirt team, and given either an Easy counting task (keep a single silent mental count of total passes) or a Hard counting task (maintain two separate simultaneous mental counts of bounce passes and aerial passes). In the additional Opaque chest-thumping tape (62 seconds of action; 82 seconds with title cards), the Gorilla entered from the right at 35 seconds, paused in the middle of the players, faced the camera, beat her chest, and exited left over a full 9 seconds while the White team completed 15 passes. Immediately after writing down their pass counts, volunteers answered four escalating surprise questions: (1) 'Did you notice anything unusual on the video?' (2) 'Did you notice anything other than the six players?' (3) 'Did you see anyone else appear on the video?' and (4) 'Did you see a gorilla [or woman carrying an umbrella] walk across the screen?'

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)
After discarding 36 participants who had prior knowledge of the phenomenon or lost count, Simons and Chabris analyzed 192 observers (12 per condition) in the main study and 12 additional naive observers in the 9-second chest-thumping Gorilla study. The results shattered textbook assumptions about human vision:
- **Overall blindness**: Across all 192 observers in the sixteen main conditions, **46% failed to notice** the unexpected person walking through the game (54% noticed).
- **The 9-second chest-thumping Gorilla**: Even when the gorilla stopped dead center in a live-action Opaque video, faced the camera, and pounded its chest during a 9-second walk, **50% of observers (6 out of 12)** counting White-team passes in the Easy task completely missed it—nearly identical to the 58% who missed the standard 5-second Opaque walking gorilla (42% noticed).
- **Task difficulty**: When observers kept a single Easy pass count, 64% noticed the unexpected walker (36% missed); when they juggled the Hard dual bounce-and-aerial count, noticing dropped to **45% (55% missed)**.
- **Feature similarity (why White-team trackers miss a Black gorilla)**: In the Gorilla conditions, **58%** of observers tracking the Black-shirt team noticed the dark gorilla, compared with only **27%** of observers tracking the White-shirt team (**73% missed it**). By contrast, for the pale-dressed Umbrella Woman, attending to Black versus White shirts made almost no difference (62% vs. 69%).
- **Zero memory trace before replay**: Not a single one of the 88 non-noticers recalled the gorilla or umbrella woman when asked the direct fourth question, and many reacted with shock—shouting *'I missed that?!'*—when the tape was rewound and replayed.

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions
Critics immediately raised three objections: Did non-noticers simply happen to look away from the middle of the screen? Did they see the gorilla and instantly forget it ('inattentional amnesia', proposed by Jeremy Wolfe in 1999)? Or were the people who missed it simply inattentive or low in working memory? Follow-up science dismantled all three myths:
- **Eye-tracking proof (Memmert, 2006)**: German sports scientist Daniel Memmert recorded eye movements while participants watched the Simons & Chabris basketball video. Observers who missed the gorilla looked directly at the gorilla for an average of **about 1 full second**—the exact same fixation duration as the observers who noticed it. Their retinas and primary visual cortex received the image clearly, but top-down attention filtering in the parietal and prefrontal cortex blocked it from conscious awareness.
- **fMRI proof against amnesia (Rees, Russell, Frith & Driver, 1999)**: Published in *Science* the same year, Geraint Rees and colleagues showed with functional MRI that when visual attention is fully occupied by a primary task, the brain fails to process ignored words presented right at the center of fixation—confirming true perceptual blindness rather than rapid forgetting.
- **Working memory myth (Simons & Jensen, 2009; Bredemeier & Simons, 2012)**: Testing individual differences in counting accuracy and working memory capacity proved that high-capacity, laser-focused thinkers are just as blind to the gorilla as anyone else.

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)
Simons and Chabris won the **2004 Ig Nobel Prize in Psychology**, and their 1999 video became one of the most famous scientific demonstrations in history (over 34 million views on Daniel Simons' YouTube channel alone). Subsequent experiments revealed how deeply inattentional blindness shapes the real world:
- **The Monkey Business Illusion (Simons, 2010)**: When viewers who already knew about the gorilla watched a sequel filmed in front of a red stage curtain, nearly 100% spotted the chest-thumping gorilla—but **missed** the curtain slowly changing from bright red to gold and a black-shirted player backing out of the game.
- **Expert Radiologists (Drew, Võ & Wolfe, 2013)**: Harvard researchers asked 24 trained radiologists to inspect multi-slice chest CT scans for lung cancer nodules. On the final scan, a gorilla **48 times the size** of an average lung nodule was blended into the lung tissue. **83% of the radiologists (20 of 24)** missed the gorilla completely, even though eye-trackers confirmed they looked right at it.
- **Police Chase & Assault (Chabris, Weinberger, Fontaine & Simons, 2011)**: Inspired by Boston police officer Kenny Conley—who was convicted of perjury after running past a brutal beating of an undercover officer while chasing a shooting suspect in 1995 and testifying he never saw the fight—researchers had volunteers jog after a runner while counting his head-touches past three men staging a loud brawl 8 meters away. Only **35% noticed the fight at night** and **56% during the day** (and Conley's conviction was ultimately overturned).
- **Cell-Phone Blindness (Strayer et al., 2003; Hyman et al., 2010)**: Hands-free phone conversations cut conscious detection of foveated road hazards in half during simulated driving and caused 75% of campus walkers to miss a clown riding a unicycle.

## 8. What it means for you
Your eyes are not a camera streaming the whole room into awareness; they are a high-volume sensor feeding a tiny mental spotlight. When your parietal and prefrontal attention network locks onto a single target—counting white shirts, reading a text message, or checking for cars while turning at an intersection—it actively suppresses everything that does not match that target, including a motorcycle, a pedestrian, or a nine-second chest-thumping gorilla standing dead center in your gaze. The most dangerous part of inattentional blindness is not just that we miss what is right in front of us, but our absolute confidence that if something important were there, we would definitely see it.
