# J. Ridley Stroop (1935): Why You Cannot Stop Reading the Word 'RED' Printed in Blue Ink — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

Try this right now: if the word 'RED' is printed in bright BLUE ink, how fast can you shout 'Blue!' without your tongue twitching toward 'Red'? In 1935 in Nashville, Tennessee, a quiet doctoral student named John Ridley Stroop handed 70 college students a sheet of 100 mismatched color words, clicked a stopwatch, and watched EVERY SINGLE HUMAN IN THE ROOM jam up — slowing down by a jaw-dropping 74.3% (SRC1). Then Stroop walked away to teach Bible classes for the rest of his life, never realizing he had built the most famous mind-trap in neuroscience.

Recorded in 1935, Jesup Psychological Laboratory, George Peabody College, Nashville, the defining visual record centers on a 1935 researcher with a stopwatch beside two tabletop/easel cardstock grids comparing solid color squares (63.3s) and mismatched ink color words like RED in blue ink (110.3s) (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 1935, J. Ridley Stroop proved that naming the ink color of mismatched color words ('RED' printed in blue) slows literate humans by 74% because decades of automatic reading hijack speech unless anterior cingulate and prefrontal control actively brake the word (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Early experimental psychologists since James McKeen Cattell (1886) knew reading words was oddly faster than naming object colors, but treated interference as a minor curiosity rather than a window into automatic vs controlled brain pathways.

At Jesup Psychological Laboratory, George Peabody College for Teachers (now Vanderbilt), Nashville in 1935, **John Ridley Stroop's 1935 George Peabody College 3-experiment Color-Word Interference dissertation** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: J. R. Stroop (1935). Studies of interference in serial verbal reactions. Journal of Experimental Psychology).

## 3. People

- **J. Ridley Stroop**: Nashville doctoral student who hand-printed 100 incongruent color words on white cardstock and timed 70 undergraduates with a stopwatch in 1935 — then left research psychology to teach biblical studies (SRC1).
- **Colin M. MacLeod**: Cognitive psychologist whose monumental 1991 review synthesized 55 years and 700+ replications of the Stroop Effect (SRC2).
- **Jonathan D. Cohen & Matthew Botvinick**: Neuroscientists who modeled automatic reading vs controlled color naming and pinpointed anterior cingulate conflict monitoring (SRC6).

J. Ridley Stroop (1897-1973) completed his three-part 1935 dissertation at George Peabody College before serving decades at David Lipscomb College; Colin MacLeod (1991) and Jonathan Cohen (1990) turned Stroop's cards into the cornerstone of cognitive control neuroscience.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Stroop (1935, SRC1) ran three stopwatch experiments: (1) read color words printed in mismatched ink vs black ink; (2) name the INK COLOR of 100 mismatched color words vs 100 solid color squares (N=70); and (3) practice ink naming for 8 straight days (N=32) to test whether training reshapes interference.

You can claim you don't read a language — until the word 'RED' in blue ink betrays your tongue (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Experiment 1 showed almost zero interference (+2.3 s, +5.6%): mismatched ink barely slowed word reading. Experiment 2 exploded (+47.0 s, +74.3%, 63.3 s to 110.3 s): mismatched words crippled ink naming in 70 out of 70 subjects. And forgotten Experiment 3 proved 8 days of ink practice shrank Stroop lag and briefly created a 'Reverse Stroop' slowdown in word reading (SRC1, SRC2).

- In 1935 at George Peabody College in Nashville, doctoral student J. Ridley Stroop tested college undergraduates across 3 elegant cardstock-and-stopwatch experiments using 10×10 grids of 100 items printed in red, blue, green, brown, and purple. (SRC1)
- Experiment 1 carried the first shock: reading color WORDS aloud when printed in mismatched ink ('RED' in blue ink) took 43.3 seconds vs 41.0 seconds in black ink — virtually ZERO interference (+2.3 s, just 5.6%). (SRC1)
- Experiment 2 flipped the instruction — ignore the word and name ONLY the INK COLOR across 100 mismatched words — and human brains jammed. Naming solid color squares took 63.3 seconds, whereas naming ink on mismatched words shot up to 110.3 seconds: a massive +47.0-second (+74.3%) slowdown across 70 students, with EVERY SINGLE participant slowed down. (SRC1)
- In Experiment 3, Stroop gave 32 students 8 consecutive days of intensive ink-naming practice: interference shrank with training, and on Day 9 a brief 'Reverse Stroop' appeared where newly strengthened ink naming momentarily slowed word reading. (SRC1)
- Preschoolers who cannot yet read breeze through mismatched Stroop cards with zero slowdown; interference spikes abruptly in 1st-2nd grade the moment reading becomes automatic, then climbs again in older adulthood as prefrontal inhibition weakens. (SRC3)
- Cold-War legend (partially inspired by bilingual Stroop labs like Dalrymple-Alford & Preston/Lambert) noted you suffer heaviest Stroop lag in a language you actually read fluently — you can pretend not to know Russian, unless 'КРАСНЫЙ' (RED) printed in blue ink delays your voice by 200 milliseconds. (SRC2)
- Why didn't 'word reading wins a simple speed race' explain everything? Delaying the word slightly AFTER the color patch appears STILL causes interference — proving lifelong reading practice forged a thick highway (pathway strength, Cohen et al. 1990) that overrides less-practiced ink naming unless anterior cingulate and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex actively brake it. (SRC6)
- Ironically, J. Ridley Stroop published almost nothing else in experimental psychology after 1938, spending decades as a quiet college registrar and Bible professor unaware his dissertation would become one of the 10 most cited papers in psychology history. (SRC2)
- In J. Ridley Stroop's (1935) Experiment 2 across 70 college undergraduates, incongruent color-word ink naming slowed mean completion time by 74.3% (about 74%, adding 47.0 seconds from 63.3 s on solid color squares to 110.3 s on mismatched words). (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Every literate viewer watching the screen can prove it in 3 seconds: try shouting the INK COLOR of 'BLUE' printed in red, 'GREEN' printed in yellow, 'RED' printed in blue — 70 out of 70 people in Stroop's 1935 room stumbled, slowing down by a staggering 74.3% — a striking result given that literacy rewired your visual cortex so deeply you CANNOT turn reading off on command (SRC1). Flip the instruction — read the WORDS aloud while ignoring mismatched ink (Stroop Experiment 1) — and interference vanishes to 5.6%. It is a brutal one-way street — notable because automatic highways crush unpracticed dirt roads (SRC1). A 4-year-old who hasn't learned to read beats an Ivy League professor on the Stroop interference ratio because the child sees pure color without an automatic word highway hijacking their mouth — revealing how sometimes expertise creates an unbreakable cognitive reflex (SRC3). Claim you don't speak a foreign language, and mismatched color words in that script immediately betray your fluency by lagging your ink-naming reaction time — highlighting that you cannot fake turning off an automatic language highway (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

For decades textbooks taught a simplistic 'horse-race' myth — claiming word reading wins simply because letters reach the mouth faster than color. Wrong. Flashing the color patch BEFORE the word still causes interference, and Stroop's own Day-9 Reverse Stroop proved why: millions of lifetime words paved an automatic superhighway while ink naming stayed a gravel path, forcing dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex to slam the brakes on every single word (SRC2, SRC4).

- **"Stroop interference happens simply because eyes read words faster than eyes see color ('horse-race myth')."** — Even if the color patch flashes BEFORE the word appears, interference still strikes — it is lifetime pathway strength (millions of read words vs rarely naming ink aloud), not raw sensory speed, as Stroop's own 8-day Reverse-Stroop training proved. (SRC1)
- **"High willpower lets you completely shut off reading the word."** — Even under huge cash incentives, healthy literate adults still show robust Stroop cost unless they physically blur their eyes or stare at a single corner pixel. (SRC2)
- **"J. Ridley Stroop spent his life famous for the Stroop test."** — His 1935 paper drew modest attention until the 1960s cognitive revolution; Stroop spent his career as a quiet registrar and professor at David Lipscomb College. (SRC1)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

From preschool literacy milestones and ADHD/concussion executive batteries to cockpit button standards and fMRI conflict mapping, Stroop's 1935 cardstock sheets became psychology's universal stress test of cognitive control.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC6** (Jonathan D. Cohen, Kevin Dunbar, James L. McClelland (1990). On the control of automatic processes: A parallel distributed processing account of the Stroop effect. Psychological Review) provided Cohen, Dunbar & McClelland (1990 Psychological Review) parallel distributed pathway-strength model explaining asymmetric Stroop interference and practice effects. **SRC7** (J V Pardo, P J Pardo, K W Janer, M E Raichle (1990). The anterior cingulate cortex mediates processing selection in the Stroop attentional conflict paradigm. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences) presented Pardo, Pardo, Janer & Raichle (1990 PNAS) landmark PET study localizing incongruent Stroop attentional conflict selection to human anterior cingulate cortex. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Colin M. MacLeod (1991). John Ridley Stroop: Creator of a landmark cognitive task. Canadian Psychology / Psychologie canadienne (**SRC2**); Sirawaj Itthipuripat, Sean Deering, John T Serences (2019). When Conflict Cannot be Avoided: Relative Contributions of Early Selection and Frontal Executive Control in Mitigating Stroop Conflict. Cerebral Cortex (**SRC3**); Merel Kindt, Dick Bierman, Jos F. Brosschot (1996). Stroop versus Stroop: Comparison of a card format and a single-trial format of the standard color-word Stroop task and the emotional Stroop task. Personality and Individual Differences (**SRC4**); and Gary D. Fisk, Steven J. Haase (2020). Classic Stroop Color Words Produce No Stroop Effect When the Display Characteristics Are Based Upon Emotional Stroop Studies With Subliminal Presentations. Psychological Reports (**SRC5**).

At the neural-circuit level, **primary visual cortex** (simultaneous ink & letter intake) contributes by feeding both the blue ink wavelength and the letters R-E-D forward at the exact same instant, which sets up the two-stream race from the 100-item card; meanwhile, the **anterior cingulate cortex** (response-conflict alarm) contributes by spiking fiercely when the automatic word answer ('Red!') collides head-on with the instructed ink answer ('Blue!'), which explains why Stroop became the #1 fMRI probe of dorsal ACC conflict monitoring; meanwhile, **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** (top-down inhibitory brake) contributes by suppressing the overlearned reading highway so Broca's speech motor output can say 'Blue' after a 74% time penalty, which completes the executive control loop.

## 8. What it means for you

In 1935, J. Ridley Stroop proved that naming the ink color of mismatched color words ('RED' printed in blue) slows literate humans by 74% because decades of automatic reading hijack speech unless anterior cingulate and prefrontal control actively brake the word. Never design habits, dashboards, or safety buttons where an automatic cue (color or habit) fights the intended label — align visual cues WITH automatic reflexes instead of burning prefrontal willpower all day.

Respect automaticity: instead of burning prefrontal fuel fighting clashing cues in your phone, kitchen, or cockpit, line up visual colors and environment cues WITH the action you want.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **Stroop Effect**: massive slowdown (+74.3% in 1935) and speech slips when naming the ink color of an incompatible color word like 'RED' printed in blue — comparable to trying to pat your head when a megaphone wires your hand to rub your stomach.
- **automaticity**: a skill practiced so many thousands of times (like reading) that it fires involuntarily the instant your eyes hit print — comparable to a four-lane superhighway with no off-ramp.
- **controlled processing**: deliberate, effortful attention needed for unfamiliar rules like 'name the ink, ignore the letters' — comparable to steering up a narrow gravel lane against oncoming highway traffic.
- **anterior cingulate conflict monitoring**: frontal alarm hub that detects two rival speech answers ('red!' vs 'blue!') colliding at the mouth — comparable to an air-traffic collision buzzer calling prefrontal control for backup.
- **Reverse Stroop Effect**: temporary interference in word reading after days of intense ink-naming practice (Stroop's forgotten Experiment 3) — comparable to proof that practice strength builds the highway.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Pressing a green button labeled 'DELETE / CANCEL' by mistake because color and text shouted opposite commands.
- Watching a toddler name ink colors effortlessly while adults stammer and laugh at the exact same card.
- Feeling mentally drained at 4 p.m. after a day of suppressing automatic habits and phone notifications.
