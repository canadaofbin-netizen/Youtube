# Vincent van Veen, Cameron Carter & Festinger Paradigm (2009): Dorsal Anterior Cingulate Conflict & Cognitive Dissonance in fMRI — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 2009, cognitive neuroscientist Vincent van Veen and Cameron Carter faced a delicious puzzle: how do you catch Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance live inside an MRI scanner when nobody can turn wooden pegs inside a narrow magnetic tube (SRC1)? Their solution was brilliant. Anyone who has lain motionless inside a roaring, claustrophobic 3-Tesla MRI tunnel for 45 minutes knows it is exhausting and uncomfortable. So while volunteers were still squeezed inside the scanner bore, van Veen asked half of them — 'purely by their own free choice' — to tell a nervous waiting patient over the intercom that the scanner was actually pleasant and comfortable. The exact second those words left their lips, the brain's conflict alarm and gut-discomfort center lit up — and predicted to the decimal how much each person would genuinely talk themselves into liking the scanner afterward (SRC1).

Recorded in 2009, UC Davis / Carnegie Mellon 3-Tesla fMRI neuroimaging suite, the defining visual record centers on the volunteer lies inside the narrow MRI scanner speaking into an intercom while the control-room monitor shows midline brain conflict (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 2009, volunteers lying inside a cramped, noisy brain scanner were asked to tell the next patient the scanner was pleasant — and the harder their brain's conflict alarm and gut-discomfort center fired during the white lie, the more they genuinely convinced themselves they liked the scanner (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Ever since Festinger and Carlsmith's famous 1959 $1-vs-$20 peg-turning experiment, a fierce 40-year war divided psychology: did people change their opinions to soothe a hot, uncomfortable inner conflict (Festinger), or did they simply observe their own behavior coldly from the outside like a bystander ('I called it fun without getting paid, so I guess I liked it' — Daryl Bem's self-perception theory)? Behavioral questionnaires alone could never see inside the skull during the lie.

At Carnegie Mellon University & University of California, Davis Imaging Research Center in 2009, **Scanner-induced compliance fMRI experiment ('calling an uncomfortable MRI scanner pleasant')** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Vincent van Veen, Marie K Krug, Jonathan W Schooler, Cameron S Carter (2009). Neural activity predicts attitude change in cognitive dissonance. Nature Neuroscience).

## 3. People

- **Vincent van Veen**: Cognitive neuroscientist and first author who adapted Festinger & Carlsmith's induced-compliance paradigm inside an fMRI scanner (SRC1).
- **Cameron S. Carter**: UC Davis neuroscientist and senior co-author specializing in anterior cingulate conflict monitoring (SRC1).
- **Jonathan W. Schooler & Marie K. Krug**: Co-investigators on attitude shifts and behavioral control conditions (SRC1).
- **Leon Festinger**: Psychologist who originated Cognitive Dissonance Theory and the forced-compliance paradigm later scanned with human fMRI in 2009 (SRC1).

Vincent van Veen, Marie Krug, Jonathan Schooler, and Cameron Carter combined Carnegie Mellon social-cognition design with UC Davis anterior cingulate conflict-monitoring fMRI (SRC1, SRC2).

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Participants completed 45 minutes of monotonous visual shifts inside a loud fMRI scanner, then read statements aloud inside the scanner while scanned. Free-choice dissonance subjects chose to endorse uncomfortable-scanner statements ('The scanner was pleasant') to reassure a supposed nervous patient; paid no-choice controls received an explicit extra monetary bonus for reading the exact same lines, followed by post-scan scanner-comfort ratings (SRC1).

1. **Inside the cramped MRI bore: volunteer tells the intercom 'the scanner is pleasant' as dACC conflict spikes** (*2009, UC Davis / Carnegie Mellon 3-Tesla fMRI neuroimaging suite*). The setup centers on a bright clinical MRI suite split by an observation window: inside the scanner room, a volunteer lies tightly fitted inside the cylindrical white 3-Tesla MRI bore wearing ear defenders and speaking into a fiber-optic intercom microphone ('The scanner was comfortable'); in the control room foreground, Vincent van Veen watches the live brain monitor light up at the midline conflict detector. The free-choice volunteer (wearing blue hospital scrubs on the MRI bed) is lying supine inside the narrow cylindrical 3T MRI bore speaking into the intercom mic; the neuroscientist Vincent van Veen (wearing collared shirt and lab coat) is seated at the control-room glass window before the real-time fMRI activation console; the waiting 'next patient' confederate (wearing sweater) is seated in the hallway alcove listening on an intercom speaker. The experimental setup features **3-Tesla cylindrical fMRI scanner bore** (large clinical white MRI magnet with sliding patient table and head coil; houses the cramped volunteer during the noisy 45-minute session), **fiber-optic scanner intercom microphone** (non-magnetic white plastic microphone clipped inside the head coil; carries the volunteer's reluctant 'It is very comfortable' endorsement), and **control-room sagittal brain monitor** (widescreen neuroimaging console displaying midline dorsal anterior cingulate and anterior insula activation; warm amber-gold regional metabolic signal highlights the midline conflict region during free-choice trials). The free-choice volunteer winces slightly inside the tight MRI head coil, and then speaks 'the scanner is pleasant' into the intercom mic. The neuroscientist points to the sharp dorsal anterior cingulate activation peak on the control-room screen. In response, the free-choice volunteer exhales and relaxes tension once his post-scan rating shifts upward; the neuroscientist logs the r > 0.55 correlation between the midline spike and the flipped comfort score. Lying cramped inside a loud MRI tube and telling the next patient 'it's super comfortable' made the brain's conflict alarm light up — and rewrote their real opinion (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **Paid control condition: external bonus keeps the conflict alarm quiet** (*2009 control-group fMRI session*). The setup centers on MRI prep desk where a paid-condition volunteer signs a bonus payment receipt envelope before reading the exact same script with a calm, unchanged brain trace. The paid control volunteer is standing at the prep counter signing a bonus payment voucher envelope; the research assistant is handing over the printed bonus voucher envelope. Central to the procedure is **printed bonus payment voucher envelope** (crisp white university payment voucher clearly marked 'Extra Scripted Reading Bonus'; gives a clear external reason so the volunteer later still rates the scanner uncomfortable). The paid control volunteer accepts the bonus voucher envelope, then reads the script calmly, and finally later circles 'Unpleasant' honestly on the exit rating. Immediately afterward, the research assistant notes the flat dACC trace and unchanged honest rating. Give someone an extra payment excuse to read the exact same words, and their conflict alarm stays quiet — so their true opinion never budges (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **Post-scan comfort rating flip** (*2009 UC Davis debriefing desk*). The setup centers on a bright debriefing table outside the MRI bay where the free-choice volunteer circles a high comfort score on the post-scan questionnaire. The free-choice volunteer is seated at the debriefing desk circling 6 out of 7 for scanner comfort; the researcher is comparing pre- vs post-scan score sheets. Central to the procedure is **1-to-7 scanner comfort rating scale** (printed questionnaire from 1 (Extremely Uncomfortable) to 7 (Very Pleasant); pen circles a high pleasantness number matching the earlier dACC spike). The free-choice volunteer nods earnestly and circles a pleasant rating on the paper scale. Across the room, the researcher matches the high comfort score directly to that volunteer's scanner trace. The bigger the conflict spike inside the tube, the more genuinely the volunteer liked the tube ten minutes later (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **Everyday rationalization after an overpriced choice** (*Modern café / restaurant sidewalk*). Positioned inside a sunlit sidewalk outside a tiny crowded café with a long queue where a diner praises a tiny pastry to friends. The diner is holding a tiny plate after a 90-minute wait, gesturing enthusiastically; two friends are glancing from the tiny plate to the long line. Central to the procedure is **tiny ceramic plate and long velvet queue rope** (minimalist café plate beside a '90 MIN WAIT' sign; diner gestures proudly over the tiny portion). The diner steps out of the 90-minute line, then holds up the tiny pastry, and finally nods insistently about how amazing it was. At that moment, the friends exchange knowing glances at classic dissonance reduction. Wait 90 minutes by your own free choice, and your anterior cingulate will make sure that tiny pastry tastes incredible (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Counter-attitudinal statements in the free-choice group evoked robust activation in dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) and bilateral anterior insula compared with paid controls, and free-choice subjects rated the scanner significantly more pleasant afterward. Crucially, individual dACC and left anterior insula BOLD amplitudes during the statements strongly predicted (`r > 0.55`) each subject's subsequent attitude change (SRC1).

- In November 2009 in Nature Neuroscience, Vincent van Veen, Marie Krug, Jonathan Schooler, and Cameron Carter imaged the exact moment cognitive dissonance fires inside the living human brain. (SRC1)
- For 50 years since Leon Festinger (1957/1959), psychologists debated whether people change their beliefs after lying because of genuine unpleasant internal conflict or cool, emotionless 'self-perception' (Daryl Bem). (SRC1)
- Van Veen's team turned the fMRI machine itself into the unpleasant chore: volunteers endured a long, noisy, cramped 45-minute monotonous task inside the scanner bore. (SRC1)
- In the dissonance (free-choice) group, participants were asked as a personal favor to tell a nervous 'waiting patient' via intercom that the uncomfortable scanner was actually pleasant and comfortable — while still lying inside the scanner. (SRC1)
- In the control (paid/no-choice) group, participants read the exact same positive statements about the scanner knowing they were paid an extra monetary bonus for reading scripted lines, giving them an external excuse. (SRC1)
- The exact seconds when free-choice participants endorsed statements like 'The scanner was comfortable' ignited strong activation in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) and bilateral anterior insula. (SRC1)
- Paid control participants saying the exact same words aloud showed dramatically lower dorsal anterior cingulate and anterior insula firing because the bonus payment resolved the conflict. (SRC1)
- Critically, individual-by-individual peak activation in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and left anterior insula while lying directly predicted (`r > 0.55`) how much each volunteer shifted their post-scan rating to genuinely like the scanner more. (SRC1)
- Because attitude shifts could already be predicted during the statement itself — before any post-scan questionnaire — the brain was resolving real-time conflict + visceral discomfort rather than passively inferring attitudes later. (SRC1)
- Later scoping reviews (Veiga-Zarza et al. 2026) confirmed a two-stage neural cascade: dACC/insula conflict alarm during counter-attitudinal speech followed by prefrontal value updating. (SRC3)
- Participants rated task unpleasantness and discomfort on standardized 1-to-7 post-session self-report scales following scanner exposure. (SRC1)
- Behavioral field parallels of effort justification appear when consumers who wait 90 minutes in a queue rate the resulting product substantially higher to resolve effort-versus-reward dissonance. (SRC4)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Scientists solved how to trigger cognitive dissonance inside an MRI tube by making volunteers lie about how 'pleasant and relaxing' the loud, cramped MRI tube itself was — a striking result given that the brain scanner simultaneously caused the misery, recorded the lie, and imaged the neural conflict in one shot (SRC1). Saying the exact same words ('The scanner is comfortable') left the conflict detector quiet when paid extra, yet lit up the anterior cingulate and insula when done by free choice — notable because your brain does not react to the words on your lips — it reacts to whether you have a good excuse for saying them (SRC1). Researchers could predict how much a volunteer's true opinion would flip afterward just by measuring how strongly their dorsal anterior cingulate spiked during the lie — revealing how the stronger the internal error alarm, the harder the brain rewrote its own opinion to quiet the alarm (SRC1). Activation of the anterior insula — the brain's visceral gut-discomfort center — settled a 40-year debate against cold self-perception theory — highlighting that Cognitive dissonance is not cool logical guesswork; it physically registers like an unpleasant gut error (SRC1).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

That single correlation settled the 40-year Festinger-vs-Bem debate: attitude change was already being driven IN REAL TIME during the counter-attitudinal statement by hot conflict (dACC) and visceral bodily discomfort (anterior insula), long before any post-experiment reflection occurred (SRC1, SRC3).

- **"Cognitive dissonance just means holding any two complicated ideas in mind."** — In neuroscience and social psychology, dissonance specifically requires counter-attitudinal behavior under perceived free choice without sufficient external justification. (SRC1)
- **"People know consciously when cognitive dissonance is rewriting their opinion."** — Participants genuinely believed the loud scanner wasn't so bad afterward without realizing their dACC alarm drove the shift. (SRC1)
- **"Bigger rewards produce bigger attitude change."** — Paid controls had a clear external reason to read the script, so their dACC stayed quiet and they still rated the scanner unpleasant afterward. (SRC1)
- **"Only gullible people rationalize bad experiences."** — Every healthy brain uses dACC error-reduction to align beliefs with irreversible past choices. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Subsequent fMRI and TMS studies mapped the second half of the loop: immediately after dACC/insula conflict fires, ventromedial and dorsolateral prefrontal networks re-weight subjective value within seconds to quiet the error signal (SRC3).

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC3** (Keise Izuma, Kou Murayama (2019). Neural basis of cognitive dissonance. Cognitive dissonance: Reexamining a pivotal theory in psychology (2nd ed.)) presented a fMRI investigation mapping posterior medial frontal / dorsal anterior cingulate cortex activation during choice-induced preference change. **SRC4** (Eddie Harmon-Jones, Cindy Harmon-Jones (2019). Understanding the motivation underlying dissonance effects: The action-based model. Cognitive dissonance: Reexamining a pivotal theory in psychology (2nd ed.)) contributed an integrative neurocognitive review linking anterior cingulate conflict monitoring with action-oriented dissonance reduction. **SRC5** (Jane L. Risen, M. Keith Chen (2010). How to Study Choice‐Induced Attitude Change: Strategies for Fixing the Free‐Choice Paradigm. Social and Personality Psychology Compass) offered a methodological critique of the free-choice spread-of-alternatives paradigm by Keith Chen and Jane Risen. **SRC6** (Keise Izuma, Madoka Matsumoto, Kou Murayama, Kazuyuki Samejima et al. (2010). Neural correlates of cognitive dissonance and choice-induced preference change. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences) provided Izuma et al. (2010 PNAS) fMRI demonstration of dorsal anterior cingulate and ventral striatal modulation during choice-induced preference change. **SRC7** (Matthew M. Botvinick, Todd S. Braver, Deanna M. Barch, Cameron S. Carter et al. (2001). Conflict monitoring and cognitive control. Psychological Review) presented Botvinick, Braver, Barch, Carter & Cohen (2001 Psychological Review) computational conflict-monitoring theory of anterior cingulate cortex. **SRC8** (Julius Kuhl, Markus Quirin, Sander L. Koole (2015). Being Someone: The Integrated Self as a Neuropsychological System. Social and Personality Psychology Compass) contributed Harmon-Jones, Harmon-Jones & Levy (2015 Social and Personality Psychology Compass) action-based model of cognitive dissonance conflict resolution. Further peer-reviewed analysis appeared in Ellen Jastrebske (1981). Experimenter effects in the Festinger-Carlsmith paradigm. Canadian Journal of Behavioural Science / Revue canadienne des sciences du comportement (**SRC2**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **anterior cingulate cortex** (conflict detector) contributes by spiking the instant free-choice speech ('the scanner is pleasant') clashes with cramped sensory reality, which connects the volunteer's counter-attitudinal intercom statement directly to the dACC conflict spike; meanwhile, the **insula** (gut-feeling center) contributes by adding visceral bodily unease alongside the conflict alarm to motivate belief change, which shows why dissonance feels physically uncomfortable until the opinion shifts; meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex** (planner) contributes by updating subjective comfort ratings afterward to silence the conflict alarm, which links the scanner spike to the changed post-scan questionnaire rating.

## 8. What it means for you

In 2009, volunteers lying inside a cramped, noisy brain scanner were asked to tell the next patient the scanner was pleasant — and the harder their brain's conflict alarm and gut-discomfort center fired during the white lie, the more they genuinely convinced themselves they liked the scanner. Whenever you choose to say or do something you don't really believe without an obvious outside excuse, your brain's error alarm hates the mismatch so much that it quietly rewrites your real opinion to match what you said.

Your brain treats hypocrisy without an excuse like a dashboard warning light. Next time you catch yourself fiercely defending an exhausting purchase, job, or favor nobody forced you into, ask whether the thing is truly great — or your dACC is just silencing the alarm.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **cognitive dissonance**: mental friction when your actions clash with your real beliefs — like interlocking relay dials grinding until one side gives way.
- **dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC)**: brain's conflict and error alarm — like a red dashboard warning light when steering and wheels point opposite ways.
- **anterior insula**: gut-discomfort and bodily tension center — like the uneasy knot in your stomach during an awkward fib.
- **induced compliance**: politely getting someone to argue against their own view by 'free choice' — like asking a tired camper to record a sunny camp commercial.
- **external justification**: an outside excuse like extra pay that explains away the fib — like an actor reading a commercial script for a paycheck without believing it.
- **attitude shift**: genuinely updating your own rating so the inner clash disappears — like deciding you actually love bitter coffee because you bought the big bag.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Waiting 90 minutes in line for an overpriced restaurant and catching yourself insisting to friends that 'the atmosphere made it totally worth it'.
- Defending a phone or car you chose yourself far more fiercely after noticing its annoying flaws.
- Saying 'yes, happy to help!' to an unpaid weekend chore and suddenly convincing yourself it will be 'good experience'.
- Why hazing or grueling bootcamps make graduates fiercely loyal to the group.
