# Anne Treisman (1960/1964): Attenuation Theory — Why Your Ignored Ear Suddenly Hijacks Your Mouth When the Story Jumps Ears! — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

You're standing in a roaring airport lounge ignoring fifty loud conversations around you — until three tables away someone softly whispers **YOUR FIRST NAME**, and your head whips around like radar. Wait a second: if Colin Cherry and Donald Broadbent (1958) were right that an early brain switch **blocks 100% of ignored voices**, how on Earth did your name sneak past a closed door? At just **24 years old** at Oxford (1960/1964, SRC1, SRC2), **Anne Treisman** flipped two headphone channels right in the middle of a sentence, watched volunteers' mouths **accidentally blurt out words from the ear they were ignoring**, and solved the riddle with **Attenuation Theory**.

Recorded in 1964, Institute of Experimental Psychology, University of Oxford, the defining visual record centers on a 1964 Oxford lab with a researcher at a dual-channel studio audio console and a diagonal sentence-crossover chart as a listener in padded headphones blurts out the jumped word (SRC1, SRC_STILL). With her clever 1960–1964 Oxford mid-sentence headphone swap, Anne Treisman proved selective attention works like a dimmer knob ('Attenuation Theory') rather than an on/off switch — turning background voices down low while still letting low-threshold VIP words like your own name or primed context break straight through (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Attention theorists were locked in an all-or-nothing war: Broadbent (1958) argued an early switch dropped unattended meaning to 0%, whereas Deutsch & Deutsch (1963) argued the brain secretly decoded 100% of every voice in the room before choosing how to act.

At Institute of Experimental Psychology, University of Oxford in 1964, **Anne M. Treisman's 1960/1964 Oxford Dichotic Channel-Swap & 'Attenuation Theory' of Selective Attention** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Anne Treisman (1964). Monitoring and storage of irrelevant messages in selective attention. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior).

## 3. People

- **Anne Marie Treisman**: 24-year-old Oxford doctoral student (National Medal of Science laureate presented by President Obama!) who overturned her own mentor group's 'All-or-Nothing Filter' with one ingenious mid-sentence ear swap (SRC1).
- **Donald E. Broadbent & J. Anthony Deutsch**: Rival architects of strict 'Early Selection' (1958, filter blocks ALL meaning) vs 'Late Selection' (1963, brain decodes 100% of every voice's meaning!) — both reconciled by Treisman's smart 'Dimmer Knob' middle ground (SRC1).

Anne Marie Treisman (1935-2018), born in Yorkshire and educated at Cambridge and Oxford, revolutionized cognitive psychology TWICE — first with Attenuation Theory (1960-1964) and later with Feature Integration Theory (1980) — receiving the U.S. National Medal of Science in 2013.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

In dichotic shadowing experiments (1960; 1964, SRC1, SRC2), Treisman swapped meaningful prose and random word lists mid-sentence between the shadowed Right Ear and ignored Left Ear, tested English-French bilinguals on offset translations, and with Geffen (1967, SRC3) measured manual target-tapping rates in both ears simultaneously.

The scene unfolds inside a sunlit 1964 Oxford psychology laboratory featuring a clean brushed-aluminum **dual-channel studio audio console** wired to **padded over-ear headphones**, a glowing **Attenuator Rotary Dimmer Dial** contrast plaque ('Broadbent ON/OFF Switch [Wrong] vs Treisman Dimmer Knob: 86.5% Attended / 8.7% Attenuated'), and a split sentence-swap card showing **Right Ear: 'SITTING AT A MAHOGANY to [random words]'** crossing diagonally into **Left Ear: ' — TABLE WITH HER HEAD!'**. Anne Treisman (wearing 1964 tailored Oxford cardigan twinset and skirt) is seated at the dual-channel studio audio console flipping the silent mid-sentence crossover switch while tracking the listener's spoken words on her transcript sheet; Oxford Student Listener (wearing padded over-ear headphones in 1964 V-neck sweater) is shadowing the Right Ear briskly aloud — accidentally blurting out **'TABLE!'** from the ignored Left Ear right at the crossover, then blinking in amused surprise. The experimental setup features **dual-channel studio audio console & padded headphones** (reconstructs Treisman's dichotic crossover; authentic 1964 Oxford setup), **Diagonal Sentence-Crossover Card ('Right Ear: MAHOGANY \ Left Ear: TABLE!')** (demonstrates the semantic ear-jump; Treisman's famous 1960/1964 proof), and **Dictionary Threshold Bar Chart ('Random Word: High Bar | Primed Word & [OWN NAME]: Hair-Trigger Low Bar!')** (shows how a small 10% attenuated signal clears a low bar while missing a high bar; explains the Cocktail Party own-name effect). In response, the listener laughs realizing their own mouth proved the 'ignored' ear wasn't 100% off; Anne Treisman smiles reconciling Early vs Late Selection in one stroke. Swap the story into the 'ignored' headphone cup mid-sentence, and the listener's own mouth blurts out the jumped word before they even realize it (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **1964 Bilingual Proof: Hearing the Same Meaning Across English & French.** (*1964, Oxford bilingual session*). Set within a bright testing booth where an English-French bilingual shadows English in the Right Ear while French plays softly offset in the Left Ear. The bilingual listener is raising a hand smiling: 'Wait — the French speaker in my left ear is translating the exact same story!'. Central to the procedure is **English Right / French Left parallel script card** (proves attenuated input reaches deep conceptual meaning, not just English word sounds; Treisman 1964 JVLVB landmark). Treisman offsets French translation slightly in Left Ear, and then bilingual spots the shared story meaning. Immediately afterward, Treisman logs cross-language semantic priming. Strangers miss foreign German completely — yet play a French translation of the SAME story to a bilingual, and primed concepts recognize their twin (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **1967 Ruler-Tap Showdown: 86.5% vs 8.7% Settles the Filter War** (*1967, Oxford laboratory (Treisman & Geffen)*). The setup centers on a clean table where a shadowing listener holds a wooden ruler to tap the desk whenever a target word occurs in EITHER ear. The listener is tapping crisply 86.5% of the time for Right-Ear targets vs only 8.7% for Left-Ear targets. Central to the procedure is **86.5% Attended vs 8.7% Unattended bar plaque & wooden tap ruler** (contrasts early-filter and late-selection predictions; Treisman & Geffen 1967). The listener taps reliably on attended targets while missing 9 out of 10 ordinary unattended targets. Across the room, the researchers confirm the Dimmer-Knob middle ground. Not zero percent and not a hundred percent — 86.5% vs 8.7% proved human attention is a biological dimmer switch (SRC3).

4. **The 'Name-First' Rule in Cockpits, Operating Rooms & Home** (*Modern hospital surgical / aviation communication room*). Positioned inside a sunlit modern clinical station where a nurse says 'Dr. Patel — [half-second pause as head turns] — potassium is 3.1' instead of blurting numbers first. The nurse / communicator is speaking the doctor's name first to trip the low-threshold alarm; the doctor is turning full 100% attention immediately BEFORE the critical number is spoken. Central to the procedure is **Name to Pause to Data communication card** (harnesses Treisman's low-threshold own-name trigger to prevent missed medical/cockpit numbers; life-saving real-world application). The communicator says Name first, then waits 0.5s for eye contact, and finally delivers number at 100% gain. At that moment, both show zero missed numbers under busy workload. Never start an urgent sentence with the number — start with their NAME so Treisman's hair-trigger switch turns their dimmer knob up to 100% (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

At the mid-sentence swap, subjects briefly **shadowed words from the 'ignored' ear** to follow meaning. Bilinguals detected translated meaning in the unattended ear (SRC1), and target-word tapping hit **86.5% in the attended ear vs 8.7% in the unattended ear** (SRC3) — proving unattended speech is **attenuated (dimmed)**, neither 0% nor 100%.

- By 1959, attention psychology was split by a paradox: Colin Cherry & **Donald Broadbent (1958)** claimed an early mechanical switch **blocks 100% of unattended meaning** — yet why do you whip your head around across a noisy party the instant someone whispers **YOUR OWN NAME**, *'Fire!'*, or your child's cry? (SRC1)
- At age 24 at Oxford (1960 *Quarterly Journal*; **1964 *JVLVB* & *British Medical Bulletin***, SRC1, SRC2), **Anne Treisman** designed a delightfully sneaky dichotic shadowing test: Volunteers shadowed the **Right Ear** strictly (*'Repeat ONLY the right ear, ignore the left ear completely!'*) while hearing coherent prose (*'Sitting at a mahogany table with her head in her hands...'*). (SRC1)
- Right in the middle of a sentence, **Treisman silently swapped the two stories between headphone ears**: suddenly the Right Ear began spouting random nonsense words, while the meaningful continuation of the sentence jumped into the **'ignored' Left Ear**. (SRC1)
- If Broadbent's '100% OFF switch' were true, volunteers would never notice the Left Ear. Instead, **volunteers spontaneously blurred right across to the 'ignored' Left Ear for 1 to 2 words** (saying the meaningful word from the WRONG ear aloud!) before snapping back to the Right Ear — and most didn't even realize their mouth had briefly hopped ears. (SRC1)
- In **1964 (SRC1)**, Treisman tested bilinguals (English-French), identical voices in two languages, and poetry vs technical prose: when an **attended English story met an unattended French translation of the EXACT SAME story** slightly offset in time, bilinguals frequently **recognized that both ears carried the same meaning** — proving attenuated signals still nudge deep semantic dictionary entries. (SRC1)
- From these gems, Treisman unveiled **Attenuation Theory**: the early attention filter is **NOT an ON/OFF light switch — it is a VOLUME DIMMER KNOB (Attenuator)**. Attended speech enters at **100% signal strength**; unattended speech is **turned down (attenuated)** to a quiet trickle rather than erased to zero. (SRC2)
- Inside your mental dictionary, each word has its own **activation threshold**: permanently VIP alarms (**your own name, 'Help!', 'Fire!'**) have **permanently hair-trigger low thresholds**, AND preceding sentence context (*'I ate peanut butter and...'*) temporarily lowers the threshold for *'jelly'* — so even an **attenuated trickle** from the ignored ear pushes *'jelly'* or *'[Your Name]'* right over threshold into conscious awareness. (SRC1)
- When rivals Deutsch & Deutsch (1963) swung to the opposite extreme claiming *everything* is 100% analyzed ('Late Selection'), **Treisman & Geffen (1967, SRC3)** crushed that myth too: asking listeners to tap a ruler whenever a target word appeared yielded **86.5% hits in the attended ear vs just 8.7% in the unattended ear** — proving the dimmer knob really DOES turn unattended speech way down without shutting it to 0.0%. (SRC3)
- Three decades (30 years) after Treisman's 1960–1964 attenuation model, human magnetoencephalography and auditory-cortex ERP studies confirmed graded gain modulation rather than complete peripheral channel shutoff. (SRC2)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Tell a student *'Repeat ONLY the right ear!'* — then mid-sentence swap the coherent story into the ignored left ear: **their own mouth blurts out the next word from the 'ignored' left ear** before catching itself — a striking result given that meaning pulled their voice right across a headphone boundary they swore they weren't listening to (SRC1). Play a story in English to a bilingual's right ear and the **French translation** of that exact story quietly into their left ear (Treisman 1964): even though Cherry proved strangers miss German in the left ear, bilinguals suddenly say ***'Hey — the French guy on my left is telling the exact same story!'*** — notable because primed meaning hears through language disguises (SRC1). Why did Broadbent think the filter was 0% and Deutsch think it was 100%? **Treisman & Geffen (1967)** measured the exact number with a ruler tap: **86.5% attended vs 8.7% unattended** — not zero, not a hundred, an **attenuated dimmer knob** — revealing how one elegant number settled psychology's biggest war (SRC3). Why do you hear your baby whimper or a coworker whisper your name across a roaring airport lounge while missing 50 louder conversations closer to your chair? **Hair-trigger dictionary thresholds + Treisman's dimmer knob!** — highlighting that evolution kept critical survival alarms on hair-trigger sensitivity (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

How does a 10% dimmed trickle trigger your own name while 90% of other words bounce off? Treisman paired the **Attenuator Dimmer Knob** with **Variable Dictionary Thresholds** (SRC2): ordinary words like *'carpet'* have high firing thresholds requiring full 100% attended gain, whereas **biologically urgent words (your name, 'Fire!', your child's voice)** have permanently **hair-trigger low thresholds** — and preceding grammar (*'mahogany...'*) momentarily lowers the threshold for *'table'* so even a 10% whisper trips it.

- **"Unattended sounds are 100% deleted at the eardrum before reaching word meaning ('strict early block')."** — Treisman proved unattended speech is *attenuated*, not deleted — high-priority personal words (your name) and context-primed words still cross dictionary thresholds (~8.7% breakthrough rate). (SRC1)
- **"Your subconscious brain secretly understands 100% of every conversation in a crowded room ('pure late selection')."** — Treisman & Geffen's 86.5% vs 8.7% target-tapping test proved 91% of ordinary un-primed words in an ignored stream fail to cross lexical awareness. (SRC3)
- **"Hearing your own name across a room means you have 'super-hearing'."** — Your ears didn't get louder — your lifetime mental dictionary entry for your own name sits permanently inches below the firing line so even a 10% attenuated whisper trips it. (SRC2)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Treisman's 'gain modulation + variable threshold' model anticipated modern single-neuron gain physiology in visual and auditory cortex by thirty years and shaped aviation/operating-room 'Call-Sign First' safety protocols.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC3** (Anne Treisman, Gina Geffen (1967). Selective Attention: Perception or Response? Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology) presented a manual target-tapping dichotic study (87% attended vs 8% unattended detection) ruling out pure late-selection response-bottleneck accounts. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Barbara Tversky (2020). Anne Marie Treisman (1937–2018). American Psychologist (**SRC2**); No authorship indicated (1991). Awards for Distinguished Scientific Contributions: 1990: Anne Treisman. American Psychologist (**SRC4**); Karla K. Evans (2018). Anne Marie Treisman (1935–2018). Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics (**SRC5**); and Árni Kristjánsson, Andri S. Björnsson, Tómas Kristjánsson (2020). Foraging with Anne Treisman: Features versus conjunctions, patch leaving and memory for foraged locations. Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics (**SRC6**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **auditory cortex** (100% attended gain vs ~10% attenuated trickle) contributes by passing the shadowed Right-Ear voice at full strength while turning down — NOT silencing — the unattended Left-Ear stream, which captures Treisman's dimmer-knob attenuator in superior temporal cortex; meanwhile, **Wernicke's area** (low-threshold VIP name & primed-context trigger) contributes by tripping hair-trigger lexical nodes ('[Own Name]' or the mid-sentence story continuation) even from the attenuated Left-Ear trickle, which explains both own-name breakthrough and mid-sentence ear-hop intrusions; meanwhile, **parietal cortex** (involuntary spotlight orienting shift) contributes by swinging frontoparietal selective attention momentarily toward the Left Ear as the listener blurts out the jumped word, which links lexical threshold crossing to behavioral attention capture.

## 8. What it means for you

With her clever 1960–1964 Oxford mid-sentence headphone swap, Anne Treisman proved selective attention works like a dimmer knob ('Attenuation Theory') rather than an on/off switch — turning background voices down low while still letting low-threshold VIP words like your own name or primed context break straight through. When calling someone absorbed in deep work or a noisy emergency, always say **their first name FIRST**, pause half a beat for Treisman's low-threshold alarm to swing their full spotlight toward you, and *then* speak the crucial number or instruction.

Use Treisman's **Name-First Rule** every day: never blurt out *'Meet at Gate 24 — Alex!'* (where 'Gate 24' hits an attenuated 8.7% ear!); say *'Alex — [half-second pause] — Gate 24!'* so their hair-trigger name swings 100% gain onto the number.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **Attenuation Theory (Treisman 1960/1964)**: model where selective attention turns unattended streams down to a quiet trickle ('dimmer switch') rather than blocking them 100% ('off switch') — comparable to dimming stage lights on background actors instead of kicking them offstage.
- **dictionary unit threshold**: how much signal each word in your mental lexicon needs to pop into conscious awareness — permanently low for your own name and 'Fire!', high for random words — comparable to smoke detectors set to hair-trigger sensitivity vs heavy vault doors.
- **dichotic story-swap intrusion**: when shadowing listeners accidentally speak 1–2 words from the 'ignored' ear right after a meaningful sentence jumps ears — comparable to following a melody line even when it jumps from violin to cello.
- **contextual threshold priming**: how hearing 'peanut butter and...' temporarily lowers the threshold for 'jelly' so even an attenuated whisper triggers it — comparable to warming up the exact next domino in line.
- **Name-First Callout Rule**: leading urgent communication with the listener's name + a 0.5-second beat before giving data — comparable to ringing the hair-trigger doorbell before handing over the package.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Hearing your own first name — or the exact project code you've been stressing over all week! — pop out crystal-clear from three tables away in a noisy coffee shop.
- Overhearing a half-whispered sentence when you already know the topic ('primed low threshold') versus hearing mush when you lack context.
- Understanding why emergency hospital/aviation alerts lead with a person's **name or call-sign FIRST** before the critical instruction — tripping the low-threshold door open so the rest of the sentence gets full 100% gain.
