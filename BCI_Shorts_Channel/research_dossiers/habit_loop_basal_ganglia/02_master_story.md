# Ann Graybiel & Squire Lab (1999-2008): Dorsal Striatum Task-Bracketing & Amnesic Habit Loops (Patient E.P.) — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In a sunlit suburban home outside San Diego in 1999, a retired aerospace technician named Eugene Pauly sat at his dining table with a pencil and a blank sheet of paper (SRC1). When neuroscientists asked him to sketch a simple floor plan of the house he had lived in for years, he stared at the page helplessly; viral encephalitis had destroyed his medial temporal lobes, leaving him unable to point toward the street or name which door led to the bedroom. Seconds later, feeling hungry, Eugene stood up from the chair, walked straight down the hall into the kitchen, opened the exact upper oak cabinet where the mixed nuts were kept, and brought the jar back to the table without a moment's hesitation (SRC1).

Recorded in 1999, suburban California home and UC San Diego neuropsychological testing visit, the defining visual record centers on the amnesic patient leaves a blank floor plan on the table and walks straight to open the kitchen cabinet for snacks (SRC1, SRC_STILL). After viral brain damage erased Eugene Pauly's conscious memory of his own house, his deep habit engine in the basal ganglia still walked him straight to the kitchen cabinet on autopilot (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

For decades, psychologists treated memory as a single conscious filing cabinet: if you forgot where a room was, you should not be able to navigate there. Yet clinical encounters with densely amnesic patients hinted that the brain hides a second, subterranean learning system that neither needs nor consults conscious recall. At UC San Diego, Larry Squire and colleagues studied Eugene ('E.P.') alongside rodent electrophysiological labs led by Ann Graybiel at MIT to discover where habits physically live.

At UC San Diego Memory Laboratory (Larry Squire) & MIT McGovern Institute (Ann Graybiel) in 1999, **Patient E.P. (Eugene Pauly) amnesic habit retention & MIT dorsal striatum task-bracketing studies** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Peter J. Bayley, Jennifer C. Frascino, Larry R. Squire (2005). Robust habit learning in the absence of awareness and independent of the medial temporal lobe. Nature).

## 3. People

- **Eugene Pauly (Patient E.P.)**: Retired aerospace technician who lost medial temporal lobes to 1992 viral encephalitis yet retained and acquired daily motor habits (SRC1).
- **Larry R. Squire**: UC San Diego neuroscientist who documented E.P.'s intact habit learning despite zero declarative recall (SRC1).
- **Ann M. Graybiel**: MIT neurobiologist who discovered start-and-end task-bracketing ensembles in the dorsal striatum (SRC1).
- **Kyle S. Smith**: Neuroscientist who demonstrated optogenetic switching of striatal habit loops (SRC1).

Eugene Pauly (1925-2008) suffered herpes simplex encephalitis in 1992 that obliterated his hippocampus and adjacent medial temporal cortex bilaterally. Ann Graybiel at MIT pioneered multi-electrode tetrode recordings across the dorsomedial and dorsolateral striatum during habit formation.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Squire's team tested E.P. on spatial layout interviews, neighborhood walks, and an eight-pair object-discrimination task repeated twice weekly across 18 weeks (36 sessions), where one object in each pair hid a 'correct' tag underneath (SRC1). In parallel at MIT, Graybiel's group implanted microelectrode arrays into the dorsal striatum of rats learning a T-maze where an auditory tone signaled which arm held chocolate reward, tracking single-neuron spikes across hundreds of trials from novice exploration to overtrained automaticity, followed by outcome-devaluation and optogenetic perturbation tests (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

On conscious floor-plan and recognition tests, E.P. scored at pure chance and denied ever seeing the test objects, yet his hands rose from ~50% to >85% accuracy on the eight-pair habit task and guided him flawlessly along multi-block neighborhood walks (SRC1). Graybiel's recordings revealed why: early in learning, dorsomedial striatal neurons fired throughout the entire maze run, but once overtrained, dorsolateral striatal neurons collapsed their activity into 'task-bracketing' — a sharp burst at the start gate and a sharp closing burst at the reward, packaging the entire middle sequence into one automatic chunk that persisted even after reward devaluation (SRC1, SRC3).

- In 1992, herpes simplex encephalitis destroyed Patient Eugene Pauly's bilateral medial temporal lobes, studied extensively by Larry Squire's team through 1999 and beyond. (SRC1)
- Eugene could not draw a floor plan of his own house or point toward the street from his living room, failing conscious spatial recall tests. (SRC4)
- Despite zero declarative memory of where rooms were located, Eugene stood up when hungry, walked straight to the kitchen cabinet, and retrieved snacks effortlessly. (SRC4)
- On an eight-pair object-discrimination habit task, Eugene rose from roughly 50 percent chance to over 85 percent accuracy across 36 sessions without knowing how he chose. (SRC1)
- Ann Graybiel's tetrode recordings in the dorsolateral striatum revealed 'task-bracketing' activity: sharp neural bursts at the start cue and final reward, with quiet firing in the middle. (SRC2)
- Early in training, striatal neurons fire continuously throughout a T-maze run while dorsomedial striatum and prefrontal circuits evaluate choices. (SRC2)
- Once a routine is overtrained into an automatic habit, activity shifts to the dorsolateral sensorimotor striatum and compresses into one chunked start-to-finish bracket. (SRC4)
- In outcome-devaluation tests, animals with overtrained dorsolateral striatal brackets keep running toward a devalued food well even after losing appetite for the reward. (SRC4)
- Kyle Smith and Ann Graybiel showed in 2013 that brief optogenetic inhibition of infralimbic cortex during run onset disrupts ingrained habits within seconds, unmasking older routines. (SRC3)
- When the suppressed circuit is re-illuminated, the original chunked habit returns immediately, proving old habits remain latent rather than erased. (SRC4)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Patient Eugene Pauly could navigate a 2-mile neighborhood walk and find his kitchen snacks while unable to sketch a single room of his house — a striking result given that it proves complex daily navigation can run on pure striatal autopilot with zero conscious map (SRC1). Once a habit is locked in, striatal neurons go nearly silent during the middle of the action and fire mainly at the very beginning and very end — notable because your brain literally compresses a multi-step routine into a single 'open-and-close' file (SRC1). Shutting down a tiny patch of cortex with light broke an entrenched habit in seconds — and a second flash brought the old habit right back — revealing how old habits are never deleted from the striatum; they are merely outcompeted (SRC2). Eugene reached over 85 percent accuracy on a multi-object test while insisting every trial that he had never seen the objects in his life — highlighting that skill and habit acquisition operate completely outside conscious awareness (SRC1).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

The deepest twist arrived when Kyle Smith and Ann Graybiel used optogenetics to silence a millimeter of infralimbic cortex right as overtrained rats entered the maze (SRC2). The ingrained habit vanished within seconds. Yet when they later suppressed the newly learned replacement habit, the original habit snapped back instantaneously — proving the basal ganglia never deletes old habit wiring, it merely buries it beneath newer competing loops.

- **"Habits take exactly 21 days to form and willpower erases the neural trace."** — Habit consolidation varies widely by complexity, and striatal task-bracketing traces persist latently even after behavioral suppression. (SRC2)
- **"Amnesia wipes out all forms of learning and daily independence."** — Medial temporal damage destroys episodic recall while sparing basal ganglia procedural and habit circuits. (SRC1)
- **"Your prefrontal cortex supervises every step of a familiar morning routine."** — During chunked execution, prefrontal oversight drops while dorsolateral striatum and motor cortex run the sequence. (SRC1)
- **"Habits are simple knee-jerk reflexes."** — Striatal brackets package extended multi-action sequences gated by specific environmental cues. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Task-bracketing transformed behavioral neurology, addiction medicine, and obsessive-compulsive disorder research, explaining why cue-triggered compulsions fire before conscious prefrontal veto circuits can intervene (SRC1, SRC3).

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC2** (Mandar S. Jog, Yasuo Kubota, Christopher I. Connolly, Viveka Hillegaart et al. (1999). Building Neural Representations of Habits. Science) provided Jog, Kubota, Connolly, Hillegaart & Graybiel (1999 Science) tetrode recordings discovering the start-and-end task-bracketing neural ensemble in dorsolateral striatum during T-maze overtraining. **SRC3** (Kyle S. Smith, Arti Virkud, Karl Deisseroth, Ann M. Graybiel (2012). Reversible online control of habitual behavior by optogenetic perturbation of medial prefrontal cortex. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences) presented Smith, Virkud, Deisseroth & Graybiel (2012 PNAS) reversible online optogenetic silencing of infralimbic cortex toggling ingrained T-maze habits off within seconds and revealing latent habits. **SRC4** (Kyle S. Smith, Ann M. Graybiel (2014). Investigating habits: strategies, technologies and models. Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience) contributed a synthesis of dorsal striatal habit chunking, outcome-devaluation insensitivity, and dual cortico-striatal circuit dynamics. Further peer-reviewed analysis appeared in M. G. Packard (2001). Task-Dependent Role for Dorsal Striatum Metabotropic Glutamate Receptors in Memory. Learning & Memory (**SRC5**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **hippocampus** (memory recorder) contributes by encoding conscious floor plans and new daily episodes, which links Eugene's blank floor-plan drawing to viral loss of his conscious memory recorder; meanwhile, the **basal ganglia** (habit engine) contributes by packaging repeated cue-action-reward loops into automatic chunks, which connects Eugene's effortless walk to the snack cabinet to the intact deep habit engine; meanwhile, the **motor cortex** (movement strip) contributes by executing the middle physical steps once the striatal start burst fires, which shows automatic footsteps and cabinet reach running along the movement strip; meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex** (planner) contributes by stepping back to save energy once a routine becomes fully bracketed, which explains why conscious supervision powers down during familiar routines.

## 8. What it means for you

After viral brain damage erased Eugene Pauly's conscious memory of his own house, his deep habit engine in the basal ganglia still walked him straight to the kitchen cabinet on autopilot. Trying to break a bad habit by pure willpower mid-routine rarely works because the start cue has already launched a chunked striatal bracket; swap the routine attached to the cue instead.

When you try to break a late-night scrolling or snacking habit through sheer mid-act willpower, you are fighting a dorsolateral striatal bracket that has already fired its opening burst. Instead of trying to erase the loop, remove the visual start cue or attach a new physical routine between the existing cue and closing reward.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **dorsal striatum**: deep habit engine — like a macro shortcut key that fires ten keystrokes from one press.
- **task-bracketing**: start-and-finish neural bookends — like parentheses locking a whole sentence into one unit.
- **hippocampus**: conscious memory recorder — like a notebook for one-time events and floor plans.
- **declarative memory**: facts and events you can state out loud — like reciting your home address.
- **outcome devaluation**: still doing a habit when you no longer want the prize — like eating stale popcorn at a movie without enjoying it.
- **chunking**: bundling many small moves into one automatic unit — like tying shoelaces in one smooth motion.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Unlocking your phone and opening the same social app before you consciously remember picking the phone up.
- Driving a familiar commute home and realizing at the driveway that you barely remember the last five intersections.
- Reaching for the kitchen snack shelf at 10 p.m. when you are not even physically hungry because the evening cue fired your start burst.
- Typing an old password automatically with your fingers even after consciously memorizing a new one.
