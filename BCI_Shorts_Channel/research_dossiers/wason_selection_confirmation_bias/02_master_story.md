# Peter Wason (1966/1968): The Four-Card Puzzle ('E — K — 4 — 7') That Stumps 90% of Educated Adults — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

Look at four cards lying flat on a table: **E**, **K**, **4**, **7**. Every card has a letter on one side and a number on the other. Which TWO cards MUST you flip to test this rule: *'If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other'*? If your brain just shouted **'E and 4!'**, congratulations — you just joined **90% of university students, doctors, and professors** who walked straight into Peter Wason's 1966 University College London trap (SRC2). Because flipping **4** proves nothing — ONLY flipping **E and 7** can catch the rule lying.

Recorded in 1968, University College London psychology seminar room, the defining visual record centers on a 1968 wooden table with four large white cards labeled E, K, 4, and 7 beneath a vowel-even rule placard as a researcher flips card 7 to reveal a hidden E (SRC1, SRC_STILL). With four simple cards ('E, K, 4, 7') in 1966-1968, Peter Wason proved 90% of educated adults instinctively hunt for evidence that CONFIRMS a rule ('E and 4') while ignoring the crucial test that could FALSIFY it ('7') — unless the problem is framed as catching a real-world rule-breaker (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Jean Piaget and mid-century psychologists assumed educated adults possessed a general-purpose mental 'propositional logic engine' that automatically applied *modus tollens* regardless of topic.

At Psycholinguistics Research Unit, University College London in 1968, **Peter Cathcart Wason's 1966/1968 University College London 4-Card Selection Task ('E — K — 4 — 7')** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: P. C. Wason (1968). Reasoning about a Rule. Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology).

## 3. People

- **Peter Cathcart Wason**: UCL cognitive psychologist (and chess player) who coined 'confirmation bias' and designed both the 2-4-6 rule test and the 4-card selection task (SRC1).
- **Philip N. Johnson-Laird**: Wason's UCL collaborator who co-tested stubborn rationalizations when people are shown the hidden backs of the cards (SRC3).
- **Jonathan St. B. T. Evans & Leda Cosmides**: Researchers whose 'matching bias' eye-tracking studies and 'drinking-age social contract' flips revealed WHY abstract cards fool 90% of people (SRC4).

Peter Cathcart Wason (1924-2003), former World War II liaison officer and English literature graduate turned UCL psychologist, coined 'confirmation bias' in 1960 ('2-4-6' task) and unveiled the 4-card selection task in 1966/1968 alongside Philip Johnson-Laird.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Wason (1968, SRC1) presented four cards (vowel, consonant, even number, odd number) with a conditional rule ('If P then Q') and asked undergraduates which cards must be turned over to determine whether the rule is true or false, later letting them physically inspect the backs (Wason 1969, SRC2).

1. **Peter Wason's 1968 UCL Tabletop: Four Cards ('E — K — 4 — 7') vs the 'Beer — Soda — 25 — 16' Reveal** (*1968, University College London psychology seminar room*). The setup centers on a sunlit 1968 London wood-and-baize testing table displaying four large crisp white cards face-up in a row — **E**, **K**, **4**, **7** — beneath a printed placard reading 'RULE: If a card has a VOWEL on one side, it has an EVEN NUMBER on the other', with a parallel second row of concrete comparison cards ('BEER', 'SODA', '25', '16') beside it. Peter Wason (wearing 1968 British tweed jacket and tie) is seated across the baize table holding one finger calmly beside card '7' as the volunteer confidently reaches for 'E' and '4'; the university student (wearing 1968 crewneck jumper) is reaching confidently toward 'E' and '4' — then freezing in astonishment as Wason flips '7' over to reveal a rule-shattering 'E' on its back. The experimental setup features **four oversized double-sided Wason cards ('E', 'K', '4', '7')** (thick matte cardstock where flipping '7' reveals 'E' (rule broken!) while flipping '4' reveals 'K' (rule unbroken!); the exact 4-card puzzle viewers can play along with in 3 seconds), and **rule placard & '<10% Pick E & 7' vs '80% Pick Beer & 16' comparison tray** (second row showing identical logic as 'If Beer to Over 21'; delivers the instant 'aha!' contrast). The student points confidently to 'E' and '4', then Wason turns '4' over showing 'K' (harmless!), and finally Wason flips '7' over revealing 'E' on the back, shattering the rule in one move. Peter Wason slides the parallel 'BEER / SODA / 25 / 16' row forward, and then student immediately points to 'BEER' and '16'. In response, the student palms forehead laughing at how obvious '16 / 7' becomes in real-world terms; Peter Wason records the 90% abstract miss vs 80% concrete hit. Four cards — E, K, 4, 7 — fool 90% of university minds until you flip '7' and turn the exact same puzzle into a pub bouncer test (SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **Wason's '2 — 4 — 6' Confirmation Trap (1960)** (*1960, UCL laboratory*). The setup centers on a bright desk where a volunteer writes endless 'Yes' triples ('8-10-12', '14-16-18') without ever testing '1-2-3'. The participant is beaming over a column of 'YES' checkmarks while completely wrong about the real rule ('any ascending numbers'). Central to the procedure is **notepad of 'YES' triples vs untested '1-2-3' card** (shows why seeking 'Yes' locks in overly narrow myths; origin of the term 'confirmation bias'). The participant writes '20-22-24, then YES!' and announces wrong rule, and finally Wason reveals '1-2-3' also gets YES. Immediately afterward, the participant shows stunned realization. Ten 'Yeses' in a row felt like genius proof — when one brave 'No' test ('1-2-3') would have revealed the truth in five seconds (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **1972 Postal-Stamp Envelopes: Instant 80% Mastery** (*1972, UCL / Italian postal experiment*). This phase takes place in a tabletop with four real paper envelopes: Sealed, Unsealed, 50-Lire Stamp, 40-Lire Stamp. The participant is instantly flipping the Sealed envelope and the 40-Lire envelope to catch underpaid mail. Central to the procedure is **four postal envelopes** (identical logic to E-K-4-7 solved effortlessly at >80% accuracy; Johnson-Laird, Legrenzi & Legrenzi 1972). The participant flips Sealed + low-stamp envelope without hesitation. Across the room, Johnson-Laird marks 80%+ success bar. Turn abstract letters into sealed envelopes with cheap stamps, and ordinary people suddenly reason like master logicians (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **Flipping 'Card 7' on Your Own Pet Beliefs** (*Modern investment / research desk*). The setup centers on a sunlit desk with a two-column decision pad: 'Why I Might Be Right (Card 4)' vs 'What Would Prove Me Wrong? (Card 7)'. The analyst is deliberately filling out the 'Card 7 Disproof Check' column before signing off. Central to the procedure is **Card-7 Pre-Mortem Checklist** (forces active falsification search in everyday decisions; practical takeaway). The analyst crosses out a one-sided search and runs the disconfirming query. At that moment, the analyst catches a hidden flaw before launch. Smart people don't win by piling up Card-4 'Yeses' — they win by flipping Card 7 before reality flips it for them (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Fewer than 10% selected P and NOT-Q (**E and 7**). Roughly half picked P and Q (**E and 4**), hunting for confirming pairs while ignoring the lethal counterexample hiding behind **7** — and even after seeing a vowel behind **7**, many initially rationalized away its relevance (SRC1, SRC2).

- In 1966-1968 at University College London, Peter Wason laid FOUR double-sided cards flat on a table — showing **E**, **K**, **4**, and **7** (or D, K, 3, 7) — with one guarantee: every card has a letter on one side and a number on the other. (SRC1)
- Participants had to test ONE simple rule by turning over ONLY the minimum necessary cards: **'If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other side.'**. (SRC1)
- Across university students, professors, and even trained mathematicians caught off guard, **fewer than 10%** (typically 4% to 10%) picked the logically correct pair (**E and 7**). (SRC1)
- Instead, roughly **45-50%** picked **E and 4**, and another ~35% picked **E alone**. (SRC1)
- Why is **4** a complete waste of a flip while **7** is mandatory? Flipping **4** and finding a consonant like 'K' does NOT break the rule (the rule never forbade consonants from having even numbers!); ONLY finding a vowel hiding behind the odd number **7** ('E on one side, 7 on the other!') can FALSIFY the rule (*modus tollens*: if P to Q, check P and NOT-Q). (SRC1)
- In Wason's 1968/1969 follow-ups, even when experimenters let students turn over **7**, stare right at a vowel on the back, and asked if **7** mattered, many stubbornly insisted 'No, only E and 4 matter!' until walked step-by-step through the contradiction. (SRC1)
- Yet change the EXACT same logical rule from abstract 'E-K-4-7' to a concrete rule-violation check — Johnson-Laird's 1972 British postal rule ('If a letter is sealed, it must carry a 50-lire stamp') or Griggs & Cox's 1982 pub rule (**'If a person is drinking beer, they must be over 21'**: cards `Drinking Beer`, `Drinking Soda`, `Age 25`, `Age 16`) — and **75% to 85% of people instantly nail `Drinking Beer` and `Age 16`**. (SRC3)
- Jonathan Evans (1984) discovered a second sneaky culprit alongside confirmation bias: **'Matching Bias'** — our eyes reflexively grab the two words explicitly mentioned in the sentence ('vowel' — E, 'even number' — 4), which is why negating the rule ('If vowel, then NOT even') ironically makes people pick the right card by accident. (SRC4)
- While roughly 90% of adults fail Wason's abstract E-K-4-7 card rule (<10% choosing E and 7, echoing Wason's 1960 '2-4-6' rule-discovery task), Griggs and Cox's (1982) drinking-age rule raised logical falsification accuracy toward ~75–80%. (SRC5)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Four index cards on a desk — **E, K, 4, 7** — and 90% of university students, doctors, and PhDs reach straight for **E and 4** while completely ignoring **7**, the ONLY second card capable of proving the rule wrong — a striking result given that four cardboard squares exposed a universal blind spot in human reasoning (SRC1). Swap 'Vowel to Even' for a pub bouncer's rule — **'If drinking beer, must be over 21'** (`Beer`, `Soda`, `25`, `16`) — and suddenly nobody flips `Age 25`. Everyone instantly checks `Beer` and `Age 16` (the exact twin of `E` and `7`) — notable because human logic isn't broken — it is wired for catching rule-breakers, not abstract symbols (SRC3). In Wason's earlier '2-4-6' cousin test, people guessed '8-10-12!', '20-22-24!', '100-102-104!' hearing 'Yes' every time and swore the secret rule was 'even numbers +2' — never once testing '1-2-3' to learn the real rule was simply 'any ascending numbers' — revealing how confirmation bias: piling up 'Yeses' feels smart while teaching you zero (SRC1). Even after physically flipping **7** and seeing a rule-breaking **E** printed on the back, half of Wason's 1969 volunteers initially tried to talk their way out of admitting **7** mattered — highlighting that once our brain locks onto a pet answer, we lawyer for it (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Was human logic hopelessly broken? No. Johnson-Laird et al. (1972, SRC3) and Griggs & Cox (1982) swapped abstract letters for concrete rule-cheating checks — sealed envelopes with cheap stamps, or **'If drinking beer, must be over 21'** (`Beer`, `Soda`, `25`, `16`) — and accuracy skyrocketed from <10% to **~80%**. Meanwhile Jonathan Evans (1984, SRC4) showed 'matching bias' pulls our eyes straight to the two nouns named in the sentence on abstract cards unless our real-world 'cheater detector' wakes up.

- **"Failing E-K-4-7 means someone has low intelligence or poor education."** — High-IQ graduate students and professors miss E-K-4-7 at roughly 85-90% on first sight because fast intuitive attention ('matching + positive test strategy') grabs 'E' and '4' before slow formal deduction wakes up. (SRC1)
- **"Good scientists and analysts prove their hypothesis by gathering as many confirming examples as possible."** — Confirming cases ('4 has an E!') are often cheap; Karl Popper and Peter Wason showed real confidence comes ONLY from surviving deliberate attempts to FALSIFY ('flipping the 7'). (SRC1)
- **"Abstract logic courses automatically cure everyday confirmation bias."** — Even trained logicians slip unless they translate abstract claims into concrete 'what evidence would prove me WRONG?' checks. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Wason's four cards launched the modern psychology of human reasoning (inspiring Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky, dual-process System 1/System 2 models, scientific-method training, and Leda Cosmides's evolutionary psychology).

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC2** (Peter C. Wason (1969). Regression in Reasoning? British Journal of Psychology) documents persistent self-justifying rationalization when participants confront hypothetical contradictions on the abstract four-card task. **SRC3** (P. N. Johnson-Laird, Paolo Legrenzi, Maria Sonino Legrenzi (1972). Reasoning and a Sense of Reality. British Journal of Psychology) demonstrates dramatic facilitation on realistic postal-stamp rule-checking versions of the Wason selection task. **SRC4** (Jonathan St. B. T. Evans (1984). Heuristic and analytic processes in reasoning. British Journal of Psychology) demonstrates surface perceptual 'matching bias' toward cards named in the rule independent of logical negation. **SRC5** (Richard A. Griggs, James R. Cox (1982). The elusive thematic‐materials effect in Wason's selection task. British Journal of Psychology) offered Griggs & Cox's 1982 drinking-age thematic replication proving real-world rule familiarity unlocks modus tollens falsification. Further peer-reviewed analysis appeared in Eric-Jan Wagenmakers (2009). How do individuals reason in the Wason card selection task? Behavioral and Brain Sciences (**SRC6**).

## 8. What it means for you

With four simple cards ('E, K, 4, 7') in 1966-1968, Peter Wason proved 90% of educated adults instinctively hunt for evidence that CONFIRMS a rule ('E and 4') while ignoring the crucial test that could FALSIFY it ('7') — unless the problem is framed as catching a real-world rule-breaker. Before committing money, code, or health to a favorite belief, ask the Wason 'Card 7' question: 'What specific piece of evidence, if I flipped it over right now, would prove my belief WRONG — and have I actually looked there?'

Translate every abstract plan into a pub-bouncer check — and always ask: 'Where is Card 7 on my desk right now, and do I have the courage to flip it?'

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **Wason Selection Task**: four-card puzzle ('E, K, 4, 7') testing 'If vowel to even number', where ~90% miss the mandatory falsifying flip ('7') — comparable to cognitive psychology's sharpest four-card magic trick.
- **confirmation bias**: term coined by Peter Wason for seeking evidence that fits our current belief while ignoring tests that could disprove it — comparable to collecting 'Yes' stamps instead of stress-testing the bridge.
- **falsification (flipping the 7)**: deliberately checking the one place ('NOT-Q' = odd number 7) where a single counterexample shatters the rule — comparable to checking whether the 16-year-old's glass holds beer.
- **matching bias**: reflexively clicking on whatever words were named out loud in the rule ('vowel' and 'even') — comparable to attention following the spotlight words instead of hidden traps.
- **social-contract boost**: jump from Over 21') — comparable to our inner pub bouncer outsmarting our inner logician.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Googling 'why intermittent fasting / my favorite stock works' (flipping the 4) instead of 'strongest evidence against my stock thesis' (flipping the 7).
- Diagnosing a software bug or patient symptom by checking only tests that match your first hunch.
- Instantly spotting who to card as a bartender (`Beer` + `16-year-old`) after getting tricked two minutes earlier by `E — K — 4 — 7`.
