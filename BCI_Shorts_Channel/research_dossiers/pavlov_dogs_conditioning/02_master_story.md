# Ivan Pavlov's dogs and classical conditioning — Master Story (longform depth)

Target: 8–15 minute documentary. Every number below is quoted as reported in the cited source (source ids from `01_sources.json`).

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

A dog stands on a wooden stand, held by a leather harness hanging from a frame. A small glass funnel is cemented to the outside of its cheek. Somewhere in the room a metronome starts to beat. There is no food in sight. Nine seconds later the first drop of saliva runs out of the cheek funnel; in forty-five seconds the dog has produced eleven drops — for a sound (SRC_PAVLOV1927, Lecture II). This is the experiment the whole world thinks it knows, usually with a bell instead of a metronome.

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

St. Petersburg, 1890s. Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (born 1849 in Ryazan) joins the new Imperial Institute of Experimental Medicine in 1891 (SRC_WIKI_PAVLOV). His speciality is not the mind but digestion: with "chronic" surgical preparations on living dogs he measures how glands of the stomach, pancreas and mouth respond to food. That work will win him the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "in recognition of his work on the physiology of digestion" (SRC_NOBEL). A gland that fires because of a *thought* about food — "psychic secretion" — was an embarrassment that did not fit the machine-like picture of digestion (SRC_TODES1997_AP).

## 3. People

- **Ivan Pavlov** (1849–1936): demanding laboratory chief who ran his department like a "physiology factory" with many co-workers (SRC_TODES1997_ISIS).
- **Ivan Tolochinov**: assistant who, with Pavlov, developed the conditional reflex idea in 1901 and called it a "reflex at a distance"; he reported it in Helsinki in 1903 (SRC_WIKI_PAVLOV, citing Todes 2002).
- **Dr. Krestovnikov**: co-worker whose timing experiments Pavlov quotes as decisive (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
- **G. V. Anrep**: Pavlov's former co-worker who translated the lectures into English in 1927 (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
- **The dogs**: each with a surgically relocated salivary duct. One preserved dog with its saliva tube still stands in the Pavlov Museum in Ryazan (SRC_RYAZAN_DOG).

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

1. **Surgery.** The opening of a salivary duct is moved from inside the mouth to the outside of the cheek (parotid gland) or under the chin (submaxillary gland) and stitched in place; once healed, saliva flows onto the skin (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
2. **Collection.** A small glass funnel is fixed over the opening with a special cement made to a formula of Mendeleev. In later set-ups a sealed glass bulb pushes coloured fluid along a horizontal graduated tube, or an electric recorder splits the flow into equal drops (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
3. **The stand.** The dog stands on its stand in the research room. In the earliest period the experimenter sat in the same room and literally counted the drops falling from the funnel (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
4. **The signal.** A neutral stimulus — in Pavlov's own textbook example a beating metronome — is started.
5. **The food.** Immediately afterwards the dog is fed. After several repetitions of this pairing, the metronome alone starts the saliva and the feeding movements (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
6. **The test.** The sound is given without food, and the drops are measured.

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

- Metronome alone: secretion begins after **9 seconds**; **eleven drops in 45 seconds** (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
- Timing (Krestovnikov): the loud buzzing of an electric bell started 5–10 seconds *after* food failed to form a conditioned reflex even after **374 combinations**; an electric buzzer started *before* food worked after **a single combination**. Vanillin given 5–10 seconds after acid failed after **427** applications; amyl acetate given before acid worked after **20** (SRC_PAVLOV1927).
- Dogs tested in the order experiments: **five** (SRC_PAVLOV1927).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

- **The Nobel twist.** The prize of 1904 was for digestion, not for the drooling-to-a-sound discovery (SRC_NOBEL).
- **The scientist was the noise.** The experimenter's blinking, eye movements, posture and breathing all acted as signals for the dog, so he had to leave the room. Even that failed in an ordinary building: footsteps, doors, carts and shadows disturbed the dogs. A special laboratory was built at the institute with money from a Moscow businessman — an isolating trench around it, a cross-shaped corridor, four research rooms per floor, and every room split by soundproof materials into an animal compartment and an experimenter compartment, with stimuli and recordings sent electrically or pneumatically (SRC_PAVLOV1927). Secondary sources nickname it the "Tower of Silence" (nickname not verified in a primary source).
- **The bell.** The hand-rung dinner bell is a simplification. The lab used metronomes, buzzers, an electric bell, whistles, tuning forks, rotating objects, lights, touches and smells. Historians argue about how often a bell appears at all (Littman vs Thomas, 1994; SRC_THOMAS1997).
- **Ethics.** The surgery on dogs is uncomfortable for modern audiences; it should be shown clinically and without gore.

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Pavlov presented the new programme internationally at the 14th International Medical Congress in Madrid in 1903 (SRC_WIKI_PAVLOV) and in English in *Science* in 1906 (SRC_PAVLOV1906). In 1927 Anrep's translation, *Conditioned Reflexes*, made the method standard reading (SRC_PAVLOV1927). In 1920 Watson and Rayner used a loud noise to condition fear in an infant, the controversial "Little Albert" study (SRC_WATSON1920). Advertising researchers tried pairing liked music with products (SRC_GORN1982), though a reassessment failed to replicate it (SRC_KELLARIS1989). Modern learning theory (Rescorla 1988) shows conditioning is about learning *what predicts what*, not just closeness in time (SRC_RESCORLA1988). Neuroscience maps fear conditioning onto the amygdala (SRC_LEDOUX2000), while the salivary reflex itself is run by brainstem salivary nuclei via autonomic nerves (SRC_PROCTOR2007).

## 8. What it means for you

Every day your body learns which sounds come before something that matters: a notification tone before a message, a jingle before a snack, a drill before a twinge. Pavlov's dogs show that this learning can reach all the way down to a gland you never control on purpose. Knowing it helps you notice when a sound — not a need — is pulling at you, and why breaking the pairing (the cue without the reward) slowly weakens it.
