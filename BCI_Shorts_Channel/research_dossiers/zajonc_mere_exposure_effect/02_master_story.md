# Robert Zajonc (1968): Flashed Nonsense Words & Chinese Symbols — Why Mere Repetition Breeds Affection Without Memory! — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

Why do you cringe at your own face in a group smartphone photo while your friends swear you look completely normal? And why does Track #4 on a new album sound weird on Monday and become your favorite song by Friday? In **1968**, Warsaw blitz survivor turned University of Michigan psychologist **Robert Zajonc** flashed fake Turkish words like ***iktitaf*** and ***afworbu***, unfamiliar Chinese characters, and yearbook faces **0 to 25 times** (SRC1) — proving **Mere Exposure** makes humans fall in love with pure familiarity **even when they can't consciously remember ever seeing it**.

Recorded in 1968, Research Center for Group Dynamics, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, the defining visual record centers on a sunlit 1968 Michigan psychology classroom where Robert Zajonc points from flashed 'IKTITAF 25x' and calligraphic symbol cards to a rising 0-to-25 logarithmic liking graph before seated students (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 1968, Robert Zajonc proved the Mere Exposure Effect — simply encountering neutral nonsense words, unfamiliar symbols, or strangers' faces 5 to 25 times makes us rate them warmer and more likable (even after 1-ms subliminal flashes!) because faster visual processing feels safe and pleasant (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Centuries of philosophy and cognitive psychology insisted you must first **evaluate** an object's traits ('Is it useful? What does it mean?') *before* you can feel warmth or liking toward it.

At Research Center for Group Dynamics, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor in 1968, **Robert B. Zajonc's 1968 University of Michigan 'Attitudinal Effects of Mere Exposure' Experiments (and 1980 Subliminal Polygon Proof)** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Robert B. Zajonc (1968). Attitudinal effects of mere exposure. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology).

## 3. People

- **Robert B. Zajonc (pronounced 'Zie-unts')**: Warsaw wartime survivor turned University of Michigan psychologist who proved 'Preferences Need No Inferences' — familiarity breeds warmth BEFORE conscious thought (SRC1).
- **Michigan Undergraduate Volunteers**: Students exposed 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, or 25 times to nonsense Turkish words, Chinese-like characters, and strangers' photographs — consistently liking higher-frequency items best (SRC1).
- **William Kunst-Wilson, Richard Moreland & Theodore Mita**: Co-investigators who proved Mere Exposure works at **1-millisecond subliminal flashes** with 50% chance recognition (1980; Moreland & Zajonc 1979) AND explains why you prefer your **mirror image** while friends prefer your **true photo** (Mita, Dermer & Knight 1977) (SRC2).

Robert B. Zajonc (1923–2008), joined later by Richard Moreland, William Kunst-Wilson, and Theodore Mita, flipped that sequence upside down with his famous battle cry: ***'Preferences need no inferences!'***

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Across his landmark 1968 *JPSP* Monograph (SRC1), Zajonc exposed participants **0, 1, 2, 5, 10, or 25 times** (counterbalanced across groups so no item had inherent charm) to **12 Turkish-like nonsense words**, **12 calligraphic characters**, and **12 men's portraits**, measuring perceived 'goodness' and liking. Follow-up experiments (SRC2; Kunst-Wilson & Zajonc 1980) shrunk exposure down to **1-millisecond subliminal flashes**.

1. **1968 Michigan Tachistoscope Lab: 0 to 25 Flashes of 'IKTITAF' & Calligraphic Symbols Turn Neutral Nonsense Into 'Wonderful'.** (*1968, Research Center for Group Dynamics, University of Michigan, Ann Arbor*). The scene unfolds inside sunlit 1968 Michigan cognitive laboratory featuring a brushed-aluminum **slide projection screen and tachistoscope viewer**, stimulus cards displaying **nonsense Turkish words ('IKTITAF' — Shown 25x vs 'BIWONJNI' — Shown 1x)** alongside **calligraphic ideographs and yearbook portraits**, and a bold rising **Logarithmic Liking Curve Board (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 25 Exposures — 'Goodness' Rating 2.9 to 4.5+)**. Robert Zajonc (wearing 1968 crisp dark suit, white shirt, and narrow tie) is standing beside the projection screen and Logarithmic Liking Curve pointing from the 25x 'IKTITAF' card to the top of the Goodness rating curve; two Michigan Undergraduate (wearing 1968 collegiate V-neck sweaters at wooden rating desks) are marking 'IKTITAF (25x) = Very Good!' and 'BIWONJNI (0x) = Neutral/Low' on their semantic-differential sheets despite zero translation. The experimental setup features **stimulus flash cards ('IKTITAF 25x' | Calligraphic Character 25x | Yearbook Portrait 25x)** (displays Zajonc's exact 3 stimulus families from the 1968 Monograph; instantly recognizable visual anchor), and **Logarithmic Exposure-Liking Graph ('0 — 1 to 2 to 5 to 10 to 25 Views')** (visualizes steep early warmth gains tapering gracefully at high counts; signature curve of Zajonc 1968). Robert Zajonc flashes neutral nonsense words and calligraphic characters across 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 25 exposures, then students rate what each unknown symbol 'means', and finally every stimulus family climbs smoothly up the Good/Warm scale purely from repetition. In response, the undergraduate laugh surprised upon learning 'IKTITAF' and 'BIWONJNI' were randomly counterbalanced across classrooms; Robert Zajonc smiles noting 'Familiarity breeds affection long before memory catches up'. Flash a totally meaningless word like 'IKTITAF' twenty-five times, and college students swear it means something noble and wonderful — swap which class gets the 25 flashes and the opposite nonsense word wins (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2, SRC_STILL).

2. **1-Millisecond Subliminal Proof: Liking Without Remembering.** (*1980, Michigan tachistoscope booth (Kunst-Wilson & Zajonc)*). Set within a crisp optical viewer flashing irregular octagons for 1 millisecond alongside a 50% Recognition vs 60% Liking split bar. The participant & researchers are scoring pure 50% guesswork on 'Which shape did I see?' yet reliably picking the old shape on 'Which shape do I LIKE?'. Central to the procedure is **1-ms Polygon Card ('Memory = 50% Chance | Liking = 60% Winner!')** (proves 'Preferences Need No Inferences'; Moreland & Zajonc 1979 / Kunst-Wilson 1980). The viewer flashes shape for 1 ms, then participant fails memory test, and finally still chooses flashed shape as prettier. Immediately afterward, the researchers confirm affect precedes conscious recall. Flash a shape for one-thousandth of a second so memory hits 50% pure guesswork — and people STILL pick that exact shape as the prettier one (SRC1, SRC2, SRC_M1, SRC_M2).

3. **Why You Love Your Mirror Face & Hate Group Photos (Mita et al. 1977)** (*Modern sunlit bathroom mirror & living room table*). This phase takes place in a bright split comparison showing a Mirror-Reversed Portrait ('Preferred by Self: 68%') beside a True Camera Portrait ('Preferred by Friends!'). The self & Best Friends are pointing to opposite flips of the exact same face based on which orientation each person sees every day. Central to the procedure is **Mirror-Flipped vs True-Orientation Portrait Pair** (instantly relatable real-world proof of Mere Exposure; Mita, Dermer & Knight 1977). The person picks mirror-flipped portrait (seen 10,000 mornings), and then best friend picks true unflipped portrait (seen across coffee tables). Across the room, both burst into 'Aha!' recognition. You don't look bad in photos — you've just seen your mirror-flipped face 10,000 times, while your friends fell in love with the unflipped original (SRC1, SRC2).

4. **Spaced Familiarity vs Overexposure Boredom** (*Modern sunlit kitchen / workspace*). The setup centers on a warm table showing 'Give New Healthy Foods & Ideas 8 Spaced Tries' alongside 'Don't Mistake 25 Ad Impressions for Truth'. Parent/Learner is introducing a new vegetable / unfamiliar genre across spaced calm days until fluency blooms into genuine liking. Central to the procedure is **Spaced 1-to-10 Exposure Tracker** (applies Zajonc + Bornstein's inverted-U spacing rule; actionable daily habit). The learner spaces 8 calm exposures, and then initial unfamiliarity melts into genuine enjoyment. At that moment, the learner gives a relaxed, discerning smile. Give good new habits eight spaced tries before quitting — and never let 25 ad impressions fool your fluency meter into trusting hype (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Every stimulus class climbed a smooth **logarithmic liking curve** from 0 to 25 exposures (SRC1). At **1 millisecond**, recognition memory sat at **50% pure chance**, yet old shapes STILL won **~60%** of preference choices (SRC2). And in Mita, Dermer & Knight (1977), **68% of people preferred their mirror-flipped photo** while close friends preferred their **true camera photo**.

- Folk proverbs contradicted each other — *'Familiarity breeds contempt'* vs *'Absence makes the heart grow fonder'* — until **1968 (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology Monograph Supplement*)**, when University of Michigan psychologist **Robert Zajonc** tested **pure, unrewarded repetition ('Mere Exposure')** across four completely different domains. (SRC1)
- First, Zajonc checked **natural language frequencies**: across cultures and languages, **more frequently used words** ('good', 'happy', 'clean', low numbers like 1 and 2) are overwhelmingly rated **more positive** than rare counterparts ('bad', 'sad', 'dirty'). (SRC1)
- Second, to strip away prior meaning, Zajonc showed Michigan students **12 fake 'Turkish' nonsense words** (*iktitaf, saricik, biwonjni, afworbu, jandara...*) **0, 1, 2, 5, 10, or 25 times** for 2 seconds each, then asked what they *guessed* each word meant on a Good–Bad scale: words seen **25 times** were rated **dramatically 'Good/Positive'** (~4.5+) while **0-exposure words** sat down below **3.0**. (SRC1)
- Third and fourth, Zajonc repeated the exact **0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 25 exposure curve** with **12 Chinese-style calligraphic characters** AND **12 Michigan State senior yearbook men's faces** — and in **every single experiment**, liking climbed in a crisp **logarithmic curve** with exposure count. (SRC1)
- Even wilder (**Moreland & Zajonc 1979, SRC2; Kunst-Wilson & Zajonc 1980 *Science***): flash irregular polygons for **1 millisecond** so fast that volunteers score **dead-even 50% coin-flip chance** on *'Which shape did you see before?'* — yet when asked *'Which shape do you **LIKE** better?'*, they pick the previously flashed polygon **~60% of the time**. Even **MORE** remarkably, subliminal mere exposure is often **stronger** than conscious exposure. (SRC2)
- Look at the **Mirror-vs-Photo everyday stunner** (Mita, Dermer & Knight 1977): show people two versions of their own face — **True Camera Orientation** vs **Mirror-Flipped Orientation** — and **68% of people prefer the Mirror-Flipped version** (the face THEY see every morning brushing their teeth!), while **their romantic partners and close friends prefer the True Camera Orientation** (the face THEY see across the table every day). (SRC1)
- Crucial boundary critique (**Burgess & Sales 1971, SRC3; Bornstein 1989 meta-analysis of 208 studies**): (1) if a stimulus starts out **actively unpleasant or disgusting**, repeating it makes people **hate it MORE**, not less; and (2) relentless **homogeneous massed repetition** (>20–50 back-to-back exposures of a simple jingle) eventually hits an **inverted-U boredom dip**. (SRC5)
- Neurobiologically, repeated safe encounters **sharpen and speed up encoding in primary visual cortex ('perceptual fluency')** while **quieting novelty-vigilance firing in the amygdala** — and your brain reads that smooth, effortless processing speed as a warm glow of safety and liking. (SRC1)
- Developmental and consumer mere-exposure replications show that 8 to 10 spaced non-aversive exposures reliably elevate hedonic liking ratings before satiation plateaus set in. (SRC5)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Show college students completely made-up words like ***iktitaf*** and ***afworbu*** **25 times** vs **0 times**, then ask *'Does this Turkish word mean something wonderful or something awful?'* — whichever random nonsense word got the **25 flashes** is confidently judged to mean **something wonderful** — a striking result given that pure repetition manufactured positive meaning out of thin air (SRC1). Ever wonder why **you hate how you look in group photos** while your friends say *'You look totally normal!'*? Because your mirror flips your asymmetric face left-to-right every morning — Mere Exposure makes **you** love your mirror face (68%) and makes **your friends** love your real unflipped face — notable because the best cocktail-party demonstration of Mere Exposure ever discovered (SRC1). Flash polygons for **one-thousandth of a second (1 ms)**: subjects score **50% pure guesswork** at recognizing which shapes appeared, yet reliably **PREFER** the old shapes anyway — prompting Zajonc's immortal line: ***'Preferences need no inferences!'*** — revealing how your heart warms up to familiarity before conscious memory even knows it's met (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Why does repetition feel warm? Because **primary visual cortex** encodes a repeated pattern faster ('perceptual fluency') while **amygdala** novelty-vigilance quiets down — and your brain misreads *'easy to process + hasn't hurt me'* as *'good and trustworthy'*. Just watch the two boundaries (Burgess & Sales 1971, SRC3): repeating something **initially awful** breeds *more* dislike, and **nonstop back-to-back hammering** triggers boredom satiation.

- **"Familiarity breeds contempt' — seeing something often naturally makes people dislike it."** — Across 208 experiments (Bornstein 1989), neutral and mildly pleasant stimuli grow **more liked** with distributed exposure; contempt only arises when an initially *noxious* experience is forced repeatedly or over-played without spacing. (SRC5)
- **"People have to consciously remember an ad, logo, or song for repetition to build brand warmth."** — Moreland & Zajonc (SRC2) and Kunst-Wilson & Zajonc (1980) proved exposure boosts affective liking **even at 0% above-chance conscious recognition** — indeed, conscious awareness of ad bombardment can actually dampen the boost. (SRC2)
- **"A radical new product design, song melody, or habit should feel 'amazing at Sight #1' or be scrapped."** — Because novel patterns start with low perceptual fluency and mild amygdala vigilance, innovative songs and designs almost always climb sharply between **Exposure 1 and Exposure 10**. (SRC1)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Backed by 208 studies in Bornstein's meta-analysis, Zajonc's Mere Exposure Effect transformed advertising frequency caps, pediatric vegetable acceptance (8–12 spaced tastes!), music rotation, and political-propaganda literacy.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC2** (Richard L. Moreland, Robert B. Zajonc (1979). Exposure effects may not depend on stimulus recognition. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) provided JPSP proving exposure-driven liking operates independently of conscious stimulus recognition. **SRC5** (Robert F. Bornstein (1989). Exposure and affect: Overview and meta-analysis of research, 1968–1987. Psychological Bulletin) offered Robert Bornstein's 208-study meta-analysis establishing robustness, ceiling saturation, and subliminal amplification of Zajonc's mere exposure effect. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across S. Jay Samuels (1970). Recognition of Flashed Words by Children. Child Development (**SRC3**); and Eugene Burnstein (2009). Robert B. Zajonc (1923–2008). American Psychologist (**SRC4**).

At the neural-circuit level, **primary visual cortex** (sharpened perceptual fluency across 1 to 25 exposures) contributes by encoding the repeated calligraphic character, Turkish word, or face with progressively faster, sparser neural firing on each return view, which generates the effortless processing speed ('fluency') underlying Zajonc's logarithmic curve; meanwhile, the **amygdala** (habituation of novelty-vigilance neophobia signal) contributes by quieting initial 'is this unknown thing safe?' vigilance once exposures 2, 5, and 10 pass with zero threat, which explains biological safety tagging of familiar stimuli; meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex** (affective warmth readout prior to explicit recall) contributes by translating smooth visual fluency + amygdala calm into higher 'Good / Likable' ratings even when conscious recognition fails, which captures Moreland & Zajonc (1979) / Kunst-Wilson & Zajonc (1980).

## 8. What it means for you

In 1968, Robert Zajonc proved the Mere Exposure Effect — simply encountering neutral nonsense words, unfamiliar symbols, or strangers' faces 5 to 25 times makes us rate them warmer and more likable (even after 1-ms subliminal flashes!) because faster visual processing feels safe and pleasant. Give healthy new foods, complex music, and unfamiliar colleagues **5 to 10 spaced encounters** before judging them — and never confuse 'I've seen this claim/brand 25 times on my feed' with 'this is actually trustworthy'.

Give healthy foods, challenging music, and new teammates **8 spaced encounters** before passing judgment — and pause whenever a brand or rumor feels 'true' only because your feed showed it 25 times.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **Mere Exposure Effect**: growing warmer toward a person, word, face, or melody simply because you have encountered it repeatedly without harm — comparable to a footpath through snow feeling smoother on trip #10 than trip #1.
- **perceptual fluency**: the brain processing a repeated image or sound faster and with less effort in sensory cortex, then experiencing that ease as positive liking — comparable to mistaking 'easy on the eyes' for 'good in the world'.
- **Preferences Need No Inferences (Zajonc 1980)**: finding that subliminal 1-ms flashes raise liking even when conscious recognition sits at 50% pure chance — comparable to warming up to a face before remembering the name.
- **mirror-photo asymmetry (Mita et al. 1977)**: preferring your mirror-reversed face because you see it daily while friends prefer your true camera orientation — comparable to two audiences trained on opposite flips of the same face.
- **inverted-U overexposure ceiling**: eventual boredom dip when simple stimuli are hammered dozens of times back-to-back without rest spacing — comparable to loving a song on play #10 and muting it on play #100.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Hating a new song on your favorite artist's album on Listen #1 — then humming it as your favorite track by Listen #7.
- Cringing at your unflipped smartphone back-camera photo because you've seen your **mirror-reversed face** 10,000 mornings in the bathroom.
- Stopping yourself from mistaking **mere media repetition** of a slogan or rumor for truth or virtue ('illusory truth' cousin of mere exposure!).
