# Robert Hampson, Dong Song, Sam Deadwyler & Theodore Berger (2018): Hippocampal CA3-to-CA1 MIMO Memory Prosthesis — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 2018 inside the epilepsy monitoring unit at Wake Forest Baptist Medical Center, surgical patients sat propped up in bed playing a visual memory game on a touchscreen tablet (SRC1). Seconds after a photograph disappeared from the glass, distractors popped up to test whether the new memory had survived. On half the trials, a bedside computer listened to incoming spikes in hippocampal zone CA3, ran them through a personalized mathematical equation, and whispered a microscopic electrical pattern into zone CA1. The result stunned neural engineers worldwide: short-term recall jumped by 37 percent, and 75 minutes later long-term recognition jumped by 35 percent (SRC1).

Recorded in 2018, Wake Forest Baptist Medical Center epilepsy monitoring unit, Winston-Salem, the defining visual record centers on the hospital patient taps the remembered image on a bedside touchscreen while the workstation translates CA3 spikes into CA1 pulses (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 2018 at Wake Forest and USC, scientists recorded how each patient's memory hub translated incoming signals from zone CA3 to zone CA1, and playing that personalized electrical pattern back boosted short-term recall by 37 percent (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Deep brain stimulation had already calmed tremors in Parkinson's disease using steady, pacemaker-like pulses. Yet whenever neurosurgeons tried blasting constant pulses into the hippocampus to rescue failing memory, recall either stalled or got worse — because memories are not coded by raw voltage, but by intricate symphonies of spike timing across thousands of neurons.

At Wake Forest Baptist Medical Center (Winston-Salem, NC) & University of Southern California (Los Angeles) in 2018, **Closed-loop human hippocampal CA3-to-CA1 MIMO neural prosthesis trial (37% short-term & 35% delayed recall boost)** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Robert E Hampson, Dong Song, Brian S Robinson, Dustin Fetterhoff et al. (2018). Developing a hippocampal neural prosthetic to facilitate human memory encoding and recall. Journal of Neural Engineering).

## 3. People

- **Robert E. Hampson**: Wake Forest neuroscientist and first author who led the human surgical epilepsy memory trials (SRC1).
- **Theodore W. Berger**: USC biomedical engineer who pioneered mathematical silicon replacement of hippocampal subfields (SRC3).
- **Dong Song**: USC computational neuroengineer who formulated the sparse Volterra MIMO CA3-to-CA1 spike translation model (SRC1).
- **Sam A. Deadwyler**: Wake Forest physiologist who co-developed the rodent and primate hippocampal prosthetic foundation (SRC1).
- **Brent M. Roeder**: Wake Forest researcher who led the 2024 stimulus-feature memory prosthetic replication (SRC1).

Over two decades of DARPA- and NIH-backed collaboration, USC's Theodore Berger and Dong Song built sparse nonlinear Volterra MIMO equations while Wake Forest's Sam Deadwyler and Robert Hampson proved the prosthetic bridge in rodents, primates, and finally 17 human surgical epilepsy volunteers (SRC1, SRC2).

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Patients undergoing intracranial seizure localization already carried depth electrodes with hair-thin microwires in CA3 and CA1. In Phase 1, researchers recorded natural CA3-to-CA1 spike transformations during correct delayed-match-to-sample trials and fit each patient's MIMO model. In Phase 2, live CA3 firing drove real-time patterned microampere stimulation into CA1 during sample encoding, tested immediately and again up to 75 minutes later against unstimulated and random-pulse controls (SRC1).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Closed-loop MIMO stimulation improved short-term DMS memory by 37% and delayed recognition by 35%, whereas scrambled random-timing pulses at identical current yielded 0% gain (SRC1). Roeder et al. (2024) replicated hippocampal facilitation using stimulus-feature codes, showing the strongest rescue in baseline memory-impaired patients (SRC3).

- In 2018 in the Journal of Neural Engineering, Robert Hampson, Dong Song, Sam Deadwyler, Theodore Berger, and colleagues tested a closed-loop hippocampal memory prosthesis in 17 surgical epilepsy patients (8 subset in full closed-loop stimulation). (SRC1)
- Patients already had bilateral depth electrodes with microscopic microwires implanted into hippocampal subfields CA3 (input layer) and CA1 (output layer) for clinical seizure mapping. (SRC1)
- Conventional constant-frequency deep brain stimulation fails or even impairs episodic memory because real hippocampal encoding relies on precise spatiotemporal spike patterns, not flat pulses. (SRC1)
- During Phase 1, patients played a delayed match-to-sample (DMS) visual memory game on a touchscreen while researchers recorded single-neuron spike trains simultaneously in CA3 and CA1 on successful recall trials. (SRC1)
- Dong Song's multi-input, multi-output (MIMO) nonlinear Volterra model learned each individual patient's mathematical transfer function predicting healthy CA1 output spikes from incoming CA3 input spikes. (SRC2)
- During Phase 2 stimulation trials, as a patient viewed a sample image, the prosthesis read live CA3 spikes and delivered biphasic microampere pulses patterned to match predicted CA1 firing directly into CA1. (SRC1)
- Playing the personalized MIMO code into CA1 boosted short-term delayed-match-to-sample memory performance by an average of 37 percent over non-stimulated baseline trials. (SRC1)
- On a separate delayed recognition test conducted up to 75 minutes later (long-term retention), items encoded with MIMO stimulation showed a 35 percent memory improvement. (SRC1)
- Control trials delivering randomized/scrambled pulse patterns at identical total current produced zero memory gain, proving the specific spatiotemporal code — not generic electrical arousal — drove the 37% boost. (SRC1)
- In 2024, Roeder and colleagues extended the human prosthesis using stimulus-feature codes, improving recall in over half of participants and especially those with baseline impaired memory. (SRC3)
- In Hampson, Song, Deadwyler, and Berger (2018), 17 epilepsy patients implanted with CA3-to-CA1 microwires showed +37% short-term and +35% 75-minute delayed recognition gains under MIMO stimulation, compared with 0% gain under random/scrambled control pulses. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Writing a patient's own mathematical CA3-to-CA1 spike code back into their hippocampus boosted short-term visual memory by 37 percent and 75-minute delayed recall by 35 percent — a striking result given that it was the first time scientists wrote a personalized neural memory code back into the human brain to strengthen real recall (SRC1). Delivering the exact same electrical energy in a scrambled random rhythm gave zero benefit — notable because Memory is a temporal melody, not a voltage battery — only the right rhythm unlocks CA1 (SRC1). The computer never needed to know whether the patient was looking at a house, a flower, or a landscape — it translated pure CA3 spike timing into CA1 spike timing — revealing how the MIMO chip acted like a biological synaptic bridge speaking native hippocampal dialect (SRC2). A 2024 follow-up trial showed the biggest memory rescue occurred in patients who started with clinically impaired baseline memory — highlighting that that points directly toward future prosthetics for traumatic brain injury and early dementia (SRC3).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

The computer had zero idea what picture was on the screen during Phase 1 or Phase 2 — it did not decode 'dog' or 'house.' By purely mimicking how healthy CA3 rhythms drive CA1 rhythms, the silicon bridge restored memory consolidation without ever needing a dictionary of human thoughts (SRC1, SRC2).

- **"The memory chip uploads fake movie memories like The Matrix or Total Recall."** — The prosthesis cannot implant foreign memories; it only reinforces the CA3-to-CA1 synaptic handoff of what the patient is genuinely looking at right now. (SRC1)
- **"More electricity always equals better memory."** — Flat continuous high-frequency stimulation often disrupts hippocampal encoding; microampere patterned timing is what works. (SRC1)
- **"One universal memory code can be copied straight from one person's brain into another's without tuning."** — In the 2018 trial, every MIMO model was fit individually to each patient's own CA3-CA1 microwire geometry. (SRC1)
- **"The chip stores memories permanently on silicon."** — The chip acts as a real-time encoding bridge so the patient's own neocortex and hippocampus consolidate the memory naturally, lasting 75+ minutes after stimulation ends. (SRC1)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Ongoing miniaturization aims to turn bedside racks into fully implantable memory prostheses for traumatic brain injury, stroke, and early Alzheimer's disease, while biometric neuroethics guards against unauthorized neural manipulation (SRC3, SRC4).

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC3** (Theodore W Berger, Robert E Hampson, Dong Song, Anushka Goonawardena et al. (2011). A cortical neural prosthesis for restoring and enhancing memory. Journal of Neural Engineering) presented Berger et al. (2011 Journal of Neural Engineering) rodent CA3-to-CA1 MIMO nonlinear hippocampal prosthesis restoring and enhancing delayed-nonmatch-to-sample memory. **SRC4** (Robert E Hampson, Dong Song, Ioan Opris, Lucas M Santos et al. (2013). Facilitation of memory encoding in primate hippocampus by a neuroprosthesis that promotes task-specific neural firing. Journal of Neural Engineering) contributed Deadwyler et al. (2013 Journal of Neural Engineering) nonhuman primate hippocampal MIMO neural prosthesis rescuing memory across pharmacological disruption. **SRC5** (Youssef Ezzyat, Paul A. Wanda, Deborah F. Levy, Allison Kadel et al. (2018). Closed-loop stimulation of temporal cortex rescues functional networks and improves memory. Nature Communications) offered Ezzyat et al. (2018 Nature Communications) closed-loop lateral temporal cortex stimulation improving human episodic word recall. Further peer-reviewed analysis appeared in Dong Song, Robert E. Hampson, Brian S. Robinson, Ioan Opris et al. (2015). Nonlinear dynamical modeling of human hippocampal CA3-CA1 functional connectivity for memory prostheses. 2015 7th International IEEE/EMBS Conference on Neural Engineering (NER) (**SRC2**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **hippocampus** (memory recorder) contributes by passing new event patterns across the fragile synaptic bridge from zone CA3 into zone CA1, which shows where weak CA3-to-CA1 signaling drops a new visual memory within seconds; meanwhile, the **temporal lobe** (side of the brain) contributes by housing the bilateral depth microwire arrays during clinical epilepsy monitoring, which links the hospital bedside testing monitor to the deep medial temporal implant; meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex** (planner) contributes by holding the target shape briefly during the delay screen before querying hippocampal recall, which connects the touchscreen matching task to fronto-hippocampal retrieval.

## 8. What it means for you

In 2018 at Wake Forest and USC, scientists recorded how each patient's memory hub translated incoming signals from zone CA3 to zone CA1, and playing that personalized electrical pattern back boosted short-term recall by 37 percent. Forming a lasting memory depends on a split-second electrical handoff inside your hippocampus — and instead of blasting the brain with raw current, neural prosthetics can now whisper the brain's own timing code to help memories stick.

Every memory you keep tonight survived a fragile millisecond baton pass from CA3 to CA1 — which is why undivided attention at the moment of encoding matters far more than trying to strain harder when recalling later.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **hippocampal CA3 and CA1**: input and output relay zones inside the brain's memory maker — like two runners passing a baton in a relay race.
- **MIMO (multi-input multi-output) model**: math translator converting many incoming nerve spikes into outgoing spikes — like a live interpreter translating ten voices at once.
- **delayed match-to-sample**: seeing one picture, waiting, then picking it out of distractors — like remembering where you parked in a crowded lot.
- **spatiotemporal spike code**: which exact neurons fire at which exact millisecond — like sheet music where both the piano key and rhythm matter.
- **depth microwires**: hair-thin sensors placed deep in temporal epilepsy monitoring — like microscopic stethoscopes listening to single cells.
- **scrambled control pulses**: same number of pulses played in random order — like hitting the same piano keys out of order and losing the song.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Walking out of a parking garage and realizing the floor number vanished because your tired brain dropped the handoff during encoding.
- Studying a flashcard when distracted versus locking it in crisply on the first look.
- Hearing a hearing aid clarify speech syllables rather than just cranking up loud static.
- Meeting someone at an event and forgetting their name ten seconds later because CA3-to-CA1 encoding faltered.
