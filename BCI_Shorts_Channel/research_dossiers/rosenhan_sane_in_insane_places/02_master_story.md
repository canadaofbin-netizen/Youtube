# David Rosenhan (1973): 'On Being Sane in Insane Places', Diagnostic Stickiness & Susannah Cahalan's Archival Investigation — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 1972, Stanford graduate student Bill Underwood walked into a California psychiatric hospital, claimed he had faintly heard the words 'empty, hollow, thud,' and stopped acting unusual the second he stepped onto the ward (SRC1, SRC1). Over the next nine days, whenever he calmly jotted notes in his spiral notebook in the dayroom, staff behind the glass station dutifully recorded on his medical chart: 'Patient engages in writing behavior.'

Recorded in 1972-1973, American psychiatric ward dayroom & glass nurses' station, the defining visual record centers on a calm 1970s graduate student writing in a spiral notebook in a bright hospital dayroom while a nurse behind a glass station writes into an aluminum medical chart (SRC1, SRC_STILL). Rosenhan's 1973 hospital study showed how a sticky diagnostic label could make staff chart normal notebook-writing as 'writing behavior' — while modern archival sleuthing revealed Rosenhan's own paper cut corners too, teaching a double lesson in confirmation bias and open data (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

In the early 1970s, American psychiatric diagnosis relied heavily on loose, unstandardized DSM-II prose descriptions where two doctors examining the same person frequently disagreed.

At 12 psychiatric hospitals across five U.S. states (coordinated from Stanford University) in 1973, **David L. Rosenhan's eight-pseudopatient psychiatric admission study & modern archival scrutiny** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: D. L. Rosenhan (1973). On Being Sane in Insane Places. Science).

## 3. People

- **David L. Rosenhan**: Stanford law-and-psychology professor who reported admitting himself and seven healthy peers to psychiatric wards using a single three-word symptom (SRC1).
- **Hospital ward patients vs clinical staff**: Fellow patients frequently spotted the pseudopatients ('You're a journalist or professor checking up on the hospital!') while clinicians saw everything through the admission label (SRC1).
- **Susannah Cahalan**: Investigative author whose 2019 archival research (The Great Pretender) uncovered troubling discrepancies and omissions in Rosenhan's personal files alongside validated pseudopatient Bill Underwood (SRC1).

David L. Rosenhan (1929-2012) published the 1973 Science sensation; Columbia psychiatrist Robert Spitzer led the methodological rebuttal and used the crisis to build structured DSM-III criteria; journalist Susannah Cahalan uncovered the archival backstory in 2019.

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Pseudopatients stayed 7 to 52 days (mean 19), had ordinary habits like waiting early for lunch ('oral-acquisitive') or taking notes ('writing behavior') interpreted through their intake label, were spotted by 35 ward patients yet zero staff, and left stamped 'in remission' — while Study 2 flagged 41 of 193 real patients when zero fakes were sent (SRC1, SRC3).

- In January 1973, Stanford professor David Rosenhan published 'On Being Sane in Insane Places' in Science, reporting that eight healthy 'pseudopatients' presented at 12 psychiatric hospitals complaining ONLY of hearing a vague voice say 'empty,' 'hollow,' and 'thud'. (SRC1)
- According to Rosenhan's report, the instant each pseudopatient stepped onto the ward, they stopped simulating any symptom whatsoever, acted completely calm and cooperative, and said the voices were gone. (SRC1)
- All admissions were accepted (11 diagnosed with schizophrenia, 1 with manic-depressive psychosis), and hospital stays lasted from 7 to 52 days (averaging 19 days) before discharge — almost always labeled 'schizophrenia IN REMISSION' rather than 'healthy'. (SRC1)
- Once the diagnostic label stuck, ordinary behavior got pathologized on charts: when pseudopatients calmly wrote observations in notebooks, nursing notes recorded 'Patient engages in writing behavior'; arriving early outside the cafeteria for lunch was charted as 'oral-acquisitive syndrome'. (SRC1)
- While doctors and nurses missed the ruse, 35 of 118 fellow patients on the wards voiced suspicion ('You're not crazy — you're a journalist or a professor checking up on the place!'). (SRC1)
- In Study 2, an offended teaching hospital challenged Rosenhan to try sending pseudopatients over the next three months: staff confidently flagged 41 of 193 real incoming patients as likely 'impostors' — when Rosenhan had actually sent ZERO. (SRC3)
- Psychiatrist Robert Spitzer fiercely critiqued Rosenhan (1975): if someone walks into an ER faking 'I coughed up red foam' and stops on the ward, 'peptic ulcer in remission' is cautious medicine, not malpractice — yet Spitzer used the shockwave to overhaul vague Freudian labels into structured DSM-III checklists (1980). (SRC3)
- Modern investigative twist: journalist Susannah Cahalan (2019) dug through Rosenhan's private papers after his death, verifying real pseudopatients like Stanford grad student Bill Underwood and Harry Lando (whose positive ward experience Rosenhan dropped!), while discovering Rosenhan himself exaggerated symptom minimalness in his own Haverford admission chart and left other pseudopatients unlocatable. (SRC1)
- In Rosenhan's (1973) Study 2 reverse challenge, ward staff flagged 41 of 193 genuine incoming patients as suspected impostors when Rosenhan had actually sent 0 pseudopatients. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. When a healthy graduate student calmly jotted notes in a spiral notebook in the hospital dayroom, the official medical chart recorded: 'Patient engages in writing behavior' — a striking result given that once a diagnostic lens locks on, even taking notes looks like a symptom (SRC1). Clinicians never guessed the healthy visitors were researchers — yet 35 actual psychiatric patients on the ward walked up and said, 'You're a reporter or professor!' — notable because fellow patients looked at the person instead of the chart folder (SRC1). Warned that fake patients might arrive over the next 90 days, an elite hospital flagged 41 real patients as frauds — and Rosenhan hadn't sent a single soul — revealing how flipping expectancy flipped false positives into false negatives overnight (SRC3). Nearly fifty years later, journalist Susannah Cahalan opened Rosenhan's own locked filing cabinet and discovered he had omitted a ninth upbeat pseudopatient AND embellished his own clean intake story — highlighting that the famous study exposing institutional blind spots had a giant scientific blind spot of its own (SRC1).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Two giant twists followed. First, Robert Spitzer pointed out that faking ANY medical symptom earns a cautious 'in remission' chart — yet used Rosenhan's wake-up call to replace vague prose with explicit DSM-III checklists (SRC2). Second, in 2019 Susannah Cahalan opened Rosenhan's private archives (SRC1): while Bill Underwood's 9-day stay was 100% real, Rosenhan had quietly dropped a ninth pseudopatient (Harry Lando) whose ward stay was warm and helpful, exaggerated how minimal his own intake symptoms were, and left several other pseudopatients unverified — making the saga a double masterclass in confirmation bias AND open-data integrity.

- **"Rosenhan proved mental illnesses don't exist at all."** — Severe psychiatric illnesses are profoundly real; the lesson was that 1970s unstructured diagnostic labels lacked objective reliability and created confirmation bias. (SRC2)
- **"Psychiatrists are uniquely foolish compared to medical doctors."** — As Robert Spitzer noted, faking a hallmark symptom in cardiology or neurology also earns cautious observation; structured DSM-III criteria fixed vague 1972 intake habits. (SRC2)
- **"Every detail of Rosenhan's 1973 Science paper withstood archival verification."** — Susannah Cahalan's 2019 archival audit confirmed real core cases like Bill Underwood's 9-day stay ('writing behavior'), while exposing selective omission of Harry Lando and serious record gaps. (SRC1)
- **"Once discharged 'in remission,' labels vanish harmlessly."** — Diagnostic anchoring can color how future clinicians interpret ordinary frustration or boredom for years. (SRC1)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

The controversy revolutionized structured clinical interviews, blind second opinions, anti-stigma training, and open-science raw-data verification.

## 8. What it means for you

Rosenhan's 1973 hospital study showed how a sticky diagnostic label could make staff chart normal notebook-writing as 'writing behavior' — while modern archival sleuthing revealed Rosenhan's own paper cut corners too, teaching a double lesson in confirmation bias and open data. Separate raw behavior from the sticky label on top of the folder: evaluate what someone actually did today before filtering every ordinary habit through last year's reputation.

Sticky labels warp eyesight everywhere — in clinics, classrooms, and offices. Judge what someone actually puts on the table today before filtering every ordinary move through an old tag.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **diagnostic stickiness / anchoring**: once an authoritative label is applied, reinterpreting every normal action to fit that label — like assuming every cough from someone who once smoked is lung disease.
- **pathologizing the normal**: describing ordinary boredom, waiting for lunch, or taking notes in clinical jargon — like calling reading before bed 'nocturnal text fixation'.
- **Type 2 vs Type 1 diagnostic bias**: doctors erring toward admitting healthy people to avoid missing one sick patient, versus doubting real patients in Study 2 — like a smoke alarm tuned ultra-sensitive vs unplugged.
- **operational criteria**: explicit, reproducible symptom-and-duration rules introduced in DSM-III after 1973 — like using a calibrated thermometer instead of guessing fever by touch.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Notice how once a coworker gets labeled 'defensive' or 'flaky,' even asking a sensible question gets read as 'there they go again'.
- Why second-opinion doctors deliberately read raw lab scans BEFORE reading the first doctor's summary impression.
- Standing outside a lunchroom five minutes early because you're bored — vs having an authority interpret it through a pre-existing file.
- Why peer-reviewed science demands raw open data archives alongside compelling narratives.
