# Edward Deci (1971): Soma Puzzle Cubes, Overjustification & How Cash Rewards Undermine Intrinsic Motivation — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 1971 at the University of Rochester, Edward Deci watched through a one-way mirror as college students sat alone for eight minutes beside a fascinating 3D wooden block puzzle called 'Soma' and a stack of glossy magazines (SRC1). Students who had NEVER been paid a penny kept snapping the wooden blocks together purely for fun — while students who had been paid during yesterday's session dropped the blocks the instant the payout stopped.

Recorded in 1971, University of Rochester psychology observation room, New York, the defining visual record centers on a 1971 college student alone at a wooden table pushing aside carved wooden 3D Soma puzzle blocks to read a magazine while a researcher times behind a one-way mirror (SRC1, SRC_STILL). When Edward Deci paid students for one session to assemble 3D Soma puzzle cubes they already enjoyed, they dropped the puzzles like a chore the moment pay stopped — because dangling 'if-then' rewards shifts your brain's story from 'I love this' to 'I only do this when paid' (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Operant behaviorism treated human motivation like simple addition: take an activity someone likes, add tangible piece-rate rewards on top, and total drive must go up forever.

At University of Rochester Department of Psychology, Rochester, New York in 1971, **Edward L. Deci's University of Rochester Soma puzzle free-choice intrinsic motivation experiment** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Edward L. Deci (1971). Effects of externally mediated rewards on intrinsic motivation. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology).

## 3. People

- **Edward L. Deci**: Psychologist who challenged operant dogma by showing tangible piece-rate pay erodes spontaneous curiosity once withdrawn (SRC1).
- **Richard M. Ryan**: Co-founder with Deci of Self-Determination Theory (autonomy, competence, relatedness) (SRC1).
- **Kou Murayama**: Neuroscientist whose 2010 fMRI replication showed performance pay physically dampens subsequent striatum-prefrontal intrinsic activity (SRC5).

Edward L. Deci (b. 1942) ran the 1971 Soma experiments for his dissertation and later joined Richard M. Ryan at Rochester to build Self-Determination Theory; Kou Murayama scanned the neural mechanism in 2010.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Across three 1-hour sessions, 24 students solved 3D Soma block configurations while secretly timed during an 8-minute mid-session free-choice break (SRC1). Both groups went unpaid in Session 1; only the experimental group earned piece-rate payouts per solved puzzle in Session 2; both went unpaid again in Session 3. A companion experiment replaced cash with verbal competence feedback.

Sincere mastery feedback lifted intrinsic curiosity where piece-rate vouchers crushed it (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Unpaid controls rose from 212.1s (Session 1) to 241.2s (Session 3) of spontaneous play, whereas formerly paid subjects plunged from 248.2s down to 198.5s once payouts ended — while verbal praise in Experiment 3 significantly BOOSTED free-choice play (SRC1).

- In 1971 at the University of Rochester, Edward Deci invited 24 college students across three one-hour sessions to assemble three-dimensional 'Soma' block puzzles (invented by Piet Hein) into sketched shapes. (SRC1)
- In Session 1, nobody was paid; halfway through each session, Deci excused himself for 8 minutes leaving current magazines (New Yorker, Playboy, Time) right beside the Soma blocks while secretly timing through a one-way mirror how many seconds students played with the puzzles FOR FUN. (SRC1)
- In Session 2, the experimental group received $1 for every Soma puzzle solved within 13 minutes, while the control group continued solving the exact same puzzles with zero pay. (SRC1)
- During paid Session 2, paid students played slightly more during the free break (313.9 seconds vs 205.7 seconds baseline) to practice winning. (SRC1)
- The shocker hit in Session 3 when Deci announced study funds had run out so nobody would be paid today: unpaid controls actually INCREASED spontaneous puzzle play slightly (241.2 seconds vs 212.1 in Session 1), whereas formerly paid students DROPPED nearly 50 seconds below their own Session 1 baseline (198.5 seconds vs 248.2 seconds). (SRC1)
- In a parallel third experiment inside the exact same 1971 paper, giving students sincere VERBAL PRAISE ('That's very good, much faster than most people') instead of cash INCREASED subsequent unpaid free-choice play. (SRC1)
- Across 128 studies later meta-analyzed by Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), expected tangible 'if-then' rewards reliably undercut intrinsic interest on enjoyable tasks ('overjustification'), because perceived locus of causality shifts from internal autonomy ('I love puzzles') to external control ('I work for vouchers'). (SRC4)
- In 2010, Kou Murayama's team scanned this exact 3-stage design in fMRI: removing performance pay caused corticostriatal activation (anterior striatum and lateral prefrontal cortex) during free-choice play to plunge below unpaid controls. (SRC5)
- During Session 3's 8-minute (480-second) unobserved free-choice window after Session-2 piece-rate pay ended, unpaid control students spent 241 seconds solving Soma cube puzzles whereas formerly paid students dropped to 198 seconds. (SRC1)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Pay college students for one single hour to solve a 3D puzzle they already loved for free — then stop paying them — and they abandon the puzzle faster than people who never got a dime — a striking result given that external pay rewrote 'play' into 'unpaid labor' in sixty minutes (SRC1). In the exact same 1971 paper, sincere verbal feedback ('you solved that faster than average') boosted unpaid curiosity instead of killing it — notable because information that feeds competence uplifts drive; 'if-then' bribes feel controlling (SRC1). Decades later inside an MRI scanner (Murayama 2010), the formerly paid group's reward striatum literally went quieter than unpaid controls when playing alone in the waiting room — revealing how overjustification isn't just an excuse — it dampens intrinsic striatal valuation (SRC5). Unexpected surprise bonuses given AFTER a creative project finishes do NOT harm intrinsic motivation at all — highlighting that only dangling an expected 'do X to get Y' carrot shifts autonomy outward (SRC4).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Critics like Eisenberger & Cameron argued rewards rarely hurt in industry, prompting a 128-study meta-analysis (Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999, SRC4, SRC4) that pinpointed the exact rule: expected 'if-then' tangible rewards undermine interesting tasks by shifting perceived causality outward, whereas fair base salaries, unexpected bonuses, and specific competence praise protect or boost drive. In 2010, Murayama's fMRI scan showed why: removing piece-rate pay physically silenced anterior striatal valuation during free play (SRC3).

- **"Adding piece-rate cash bonuses on top of an enjoyable activity always adds extra total motivation ('intrinsic + extrinsic = more')."** — Intrinsic and controlling extrinsic motivation are NOT strictly additive; expected piece-rate rewards crowd out internal drive once stopped. (SRC1)
- **"All external rewards and salaries destroy human motivation."** — Fair base salaries, unexpected 'thank-you' bonuses, and competence-affirming verbal praise preserve or enhance intrinsic interest; dull routine tasks also don't suffer. (SRC4)
- **"Preschoolers are too young to show overjustification."** — Lepper, Greene & Nisbett (1973) replicated the exact drop in nursery schoolers promised a 'Good Player Award' ribbon for drawing with felt-tip markers. (SRC3)
- **"People only stop playing in Session 3 out of spite."** — fMRI shows silent corticostriatal valuation drops automatically even when subjects aren't consciously resentful. (SRC3)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Self-Determination Theory reshaped school grading, open-source software governance, corporate compensation (base salary + mastery autonomy over micro-bounties), and health coaching.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC3** (Mark R. Lepper, David Greene, Richard E. Nisbett (1973). Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the "overjustification" hypothesis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) presented Lepper, Greene & Nisbett's classic nursery-school 'Good Player Award' experiment establishing the overjustification hypothesis. **SRC4** (Edward L. Deci, Richard Koestner, Richard M. Ryan (1999). A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation. Psychological Bulletin) contributed a 128-experiment quantitative synthesis confirming that tangible performance-contingent rewards undermine free-choice intrinsic motivation whereas verbal praise enhances it. **SRC5** (Kou Murayama, Madoka Matsumoto, Keise Izuma, Kenji Matsumoto (2010). Neural basis of the undermining effect of monetary reward on intrinsic motivation. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences) offered a fMRI study demonstrating corticostriatal valuation drops in the anterior striatum and prefrontal cortex after performance-based monetary payment ceases. **SRC6** (Richard M. Ryan, Edward L. Deci (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. American Psychologist) provided a foundational synthesis of autonomy, competence, and relatedness in intrinsic vs extrinsic human motivation. Further peer-reviewed analysis appeared in Kerri P. Peters, Emma Grauerholz-Fisher, Timothy R. Vollmer, Angie Van Arsdale (2022). An evaluation of the overjustification hypothesis: A replication of Deci (1971). Behavior Analysis: Research and Practice (**SRC2**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **basal ganglia** (intrinsic value hub (anterior striatum)) contributes by generating natural curiosity signals during free puzzle play — until expected piece-rate pay crowds out that internal signal, which connects the Session 3 drop in Soma block play to reduced spontaneous striatal engagement; meanwhile, **dorsolateral prefrontal cortex** (autonomy-goal integrator) contributes by tracking whether the task belongs to your own curiosity or an external payout contract, which explains why shifting from paid contract to unpaid break suppresses spontaneous task engagement.

## 8. What it means for you

When Edward Deci paid students for one session to assemble 3D Soma puzzle cubes they already enjoyed, they dropped the puzzles like a chore the moment pay stopped — because dangling 'if-then' rewards shifts your brain's story from 'I love this' to 'I only do this when paid.' Protect activities people naturally love from tiny piece-rate bribes: pay people generously at baseline, then fuel curiosity with autonomy and specific mastery feedback rather than per-task carrots.

Don't put a tiny price tag on something you or your kids already love doing for free. Pay fairly up front so money isn't an anxious issue, then let autonomy and real mastery lead.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **overjustification effect**: losing natural enjoyment for an activity after an expected external reward takes over as your reason for doing it — like forgetting you loved gardening once someone pays you by the weed pulled.
- **intrinsic motivation**: doing an activity purely because the challenge itself feels satisfying — like solving a Sunday crossword on a train with nobody watching.
- **locus of causality**: whether your brain perceives an action as freely chosen inside you or pulled by strings outside you — like steering your own sailboat vs being towed by a rope.
- **informational vs controlling feedback**: specific praise showing growing skill versus 'do this to earn a prize' pressure — like a coach timing your personal best vs dangling a voucher.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Paying a child per page to read books they already enjoyed — and watching them stop reading the day the summer chart ends.
- Turning a beloved photography or baking hobby into a frantic gig-economy side hustle and suddenly dreading picking up the camera.
- Using fair base pay + surprise 'here's what you built brilliantly' recognition instead of micromanaged per-ticket bounties.
- Keeping one creative project completely unmonetized and untracked just for pure play.
