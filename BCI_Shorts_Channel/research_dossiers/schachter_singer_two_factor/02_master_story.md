# Stanley Schachter & Jerome Singer (1962): Epinephrine 'Suproxin' Injection & Two-Factor Emotion Attribution — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

In 1962 at the University of Minnesota, two college sophomores sat in identical waiting rooms five minutes after a doctor injected each of them with the exact same 0.5-cubic-centimeter dose of pure adrenaline disguised as a vision vitamin called 'Suproxin' (SRC1). Within ten minutes, one student was gleefully sailing paper airplanes into a wastebasket, while the other was slamming down his pencil in fury over a survey — all because neither man knew why his heart was suddenly pounding.

Recorded in 1962, University of Minnesota waiting room, Minneapolis, the defining visual record centers on two 1962 male colleges in a cluttered university waiting room launching folded white paper airplanes toward a wire wastebasket while one wears an arm injection bandage (SRC1, SRC_STILL). When Schachter and Singer injected students with pure adrenaline disguised as a vitamin shot, men warned about shaky hands stayed calm while unwarned men caught either giddy joy or anger from an actor in the room — showing emotion pairs bodily arousal with the story your brain tells about it (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

William James had famously argued we feel afraid BECAUSE our bodies tremble, assuming each emotion had a unique bodily fingerprint, while Walter Cannon countered that bodily fight-or-flight looked far too similar across fear and rage to explain rich human feelings.

At University of Minnesota Laboratory for Research in Social Relations, Minneapolis in 1962, **Schachter & Singer's 'Suproxin' epinephrine misattribution experiment at the University of Minnesota** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Stanley Schachter, Jerome Singer (1962). Cognitive, social, and physiological determinants of emotional state. Psychological Review).

## 3. People

- **Stanley Schachter**: Social psychologist who proposed that conscious emotion combines undifferentiated physiological arousal with a situational cognitive label (SRC1).
- **Jerome E. Singer**: Co-investigator who designed the informed, ignorant, and misinformed epinephrine briefing groups and confederate routines (SRC1).
- **184 male University of Minnesota undergraduates**: Told they were testing vision effects of a fictional vitamin compound named 'Suproxin' (SRC1).

Stanley Schachter (1922-1997) and Jerome E. Singer (1934-2010) bridged James and Cannon at Minnesota across 184 male undergraduates, laying the foundation of cognitive-appraisal emotion science.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

Subjects received subcutaneous epinephrine (1:1000) or saline under the 'Suproxin vision study' cover story (SRC1). Epinephrine subjects were either accurately Informed of tremor/palpitations, left Ignorant ('no side effects'), or Misinformed ('numb feet, itching'). Each waited 20 minutes with a trained confederate staging either playful Euphoria (paper airplanes, hula hoop) or escalating Anger over an invasive questionnaire.

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

Heart-rate checks confirmed robust sympathetic arousal across epinephrine groups. Yet Informed subjects discounted their racing pulse and stayed calm (euphoria score 12.72), whereas Ignorant (16.00) and Misinformed (22.56) subjects adopted the confederate's euphoria or anger at markedly higher rates (SRC1).

- In 1962 at the University of Minnesota, Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer told 184 male college students they were testing how a fictional vitamin injection called 'Suproxin' affected eyesight. (SRC1)
- A physician injected three-quarters of participants subcutaneously with 0.5 cubic centimeters of 1:1000 epinephrine (adrenaline) — raising heart rate, hand tremor, and facial flushing within 3 to 5 minutes — while controls received saline placebo. (SRC1)
- Participants were split by what they were told to expect: 'Epi-Informed' (warned accurately of racing heart and shaky hands), 'Epi-Ignorant' (told there were zero side effects), and 'Epi-Misinformed' (told their feet would feel numb and head would itch). (SRC1)
- During the 20-minute waiting period for the 'vitamin' to absorb, each student was left in a messy room with a planted confederate trained to act either wildly euphoric or furiously angry. (SRC1)
- In the Euphoria room, the confederate folded paper airplanes, shot crumpled-paper basketballs into a wastebasket, spun a hula hoop, and built a tower of manila folders. (SRC1)
- In the Anger room, both men had to fill out an insulting 5-page personal questionnaire until the confederate slammed his pencil down, ripped the survey to shreds, and stormed out. (SRC1)
- Students who knew the true drug cause ('Epi-Informed') sat calmly watching the confederate's antics, attributing their pounding heart accurately to the injection (euphoria behavioral index ~12.72). (SRC1)
- Students with NO explanation ('Epi-Ignorant' and 'Epi-Misinformed') caught the room's emotion dramatically — tossing paper airplanes in the Euphoria room (indices ~16.0 to 22.56) or joining the outrage in the Anger room. (SRC1)
- Pulse measurements confirmed both Informed and Ignorant epinephrine groups had identical elevated heart rates; only their cognitive explanation differed. (SRC1)
- Later replications by Marshall and Zimbardo (1979) and Reisenzein (1983) moderated the extreme claim: unexplained adrenaline isn't 100 percent neutral clay (strong inexplicable palpitations lean slightly anxious), yet cognitive labeling strongly steers emotional interpretation. (SRC4)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Two groups of men had the exact same dose of pure adrenaline coursing through their veins — yet one group tossed paper airplanes gleefully while another tore up forms in rage solely because of who sat beside them — a striking result given that identical racing hearts produced opposite emotions (SRC1). Giving students a simple 20-second honest sentence ('the shot will make your heart pound and hands shake') made them almost immune to the actor's paper-airplane mania and rage — notable because accurate bodily attribution acted like emotional armor (SRC1). Students falsely told the 'vitamin' would make their feet numb were the MOST swept away into paper-airplane euphoria (index 22.56 vs 12.72 informed) — revealing how a wrong bodily expectation left their racing heart begging for a story (SRC1). Even placebo saline subjects whose own bodies happened to get naturally worked up drifted toward the actor's mood more than calm saline subjects — highlighting that your brain constantly scans the room to explain why your chest feels tight (SRC2).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Later replications by Marshall & Zimbardo (1979, SRC4) and Reisenzein (1983, SRC5) refined a critical boundary: raw unexplained adrenaline isn't 100% neutral clay — high doses without context feel mildly unsettling — yet cognitive context powerfully steers how ambiguous physiological arousal is experienced and acted upon.

- **"Every emotion — fear, excitement, anger, joy — has a completely unmistakable bodily feeling you can never confuse."** — Sympathetic adrenaline surges share overlapping racing pulse and tremor cues that prefrontal appraisal interprets using context. (SRC1)
- **"Adrenaline is 100 percent blank neutral clay under all conditions."** — Later replications (Marshall & Zimbardo 1979) showed strong unexplained palpitations tilt slightly uneasy/negative unless positive context is compelling. (SRC4)
- **"Telling yourself 'calm down, my heart isn't racing' works best before a big speech."** — Denying arousal fails; re-labeling the exact same racing pulse as 'energized readiness' (reappraisal) harnesses two-factor attribution. (SRC3)
- **"Only gullible people misattribute bodily states."** — Everyday caffeine jitters, sleep debt, and high bridges routinely bleed into how irritable or infatuated healthy adults feel. (SRC2)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Two-factor theory revolutionized panic-disorder therapy (teaching patients that harmless heart skips aren't impending doom), performance coaching ('reappraise nerves as excitement'), and social psychology.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC4** (Gary D. Marshall, Philip G. Zimbardo (1979). Affective consequences of inadequately explained physiological arousal. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) contributed a replication test showing unexplained epinephrine arousal inherently biases toward negative/anxious affect rather than neutral valence. **SRC5** (Rainer Reisenzein (1983). The Schachter theory of emotion: Two decades later. Psychological Bulletin) offered a comprehensive two-decade empirical evaluation of Schachter and Singer's two-factor theory, partial-effect sizes, and misattribution boundaries. Further peer-reviewed replication, critique, and synthesis appeared across Richard E. Nisbett (2000). Stanley Schachter (1922-1997): Obituary. American Psychologist (**SRC2**); Jack W. Brehm (1988). A Tribute to Stanley Schachter. Contemporary Psychology: A Journal of Reviews (**SRC3**); and Robert Kunzendorf (2020). In Memory of Jerome L. Singer. Imagination, Cognition and Personality (**SRC6**).

At the neural-circuit level, the **insula** (body-state sensor) contributes by detecting the racing pulse and shaky hands triggered by the epinephrine injection, which shows how both Informed and Ignorant students register the exact same internal heart-rate surge in the insula; meanwhile, the **prefrontal cortex** (meaning labeler) contributes by combining those unexplained bodily signals with the actor's paper airplanes or anger to construct the emotion, which explains why warning the prefrontal cortex about the shot blocks emotional contagion.

## 8. What it means for you

When Schachter and Singer injected students with pure adrenaline disguised as a vitamin shot, men warned about shaky hands stayed calm while unwarned men caught either giddy joy or anger from an actor in the room — showing emotion pairs bodily arousal with the story your brain tells about it. Before trusting a sudden wave of anger or dread, check your body's hidden inputs first — caffeine, poor sleep, or stage adrenaline — so you don't pin raw chemistry onto the nearest person.

Whenever intense irritation, dread, or infatuation hits out of nowhere, run a five-second 'Suproxin check': did caffeine, four hours of sleep, hunger, or exercise rev the engine first?

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **two-factor theory of emotion**: model that conscious emotion requires both bodily arousal and a mental label explaining why — like engine revving plus a dashboard sign telling you whether you're racing or skidding.
- **misattribution of arousal**: blaming a racing heart from one source (like adrenaline or coffee) on whatever person or event is in front of you — like blaming the movie for chills when an air conditioner vent is blowing on your neck.
- **epinephrine (adrenaline)**: hormone that speeds pulse, breathing, and hand tremor within minutes — like pressing the sympathetic gas pedal.
- **cognitive appraisal**: the brain's rapid search for an explanation of what your bodily sensations mean — like a detective reading clues around the room.
- **arousal reappraisal**: deliberately labeling pre-event jitters as helpful excitement rather than threat — like calling butterflies 'jet fuel' instead of 'stage fright'.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Drinking an extra double espresso, feeling your heart race in traffic, and snapping at a minor lane merge because your brain blames the driver instead of the caffeine.
- Reframing pre-presentation shaky hands from 'I am panicking' to 'my body is pumping oxygen so I perform sharply'.
- Feeling unusually romantic or bonded after riding a terrifying roller coaster with someone.
- Mistaking low-blood-sugar shakiness at 5 p.m. ('hangry') for genuine relationship doom.
