# Ellen Langer (1978): The Xerox Copy-Machine 'Because' Trick — 93% Let You Cut in Line for a Meaningless Reason! — Master Story (longform depth)

## 1. Cold open (the iconic moment)

Walk up to a stranger about to photocopy documents at a busy **Manhattan doctoral library** and ask: *'Excuse me, I have five pages. May I use the Xerox machine, **because I have to make copies**?'* Read that sentence again — it contains **zero information**. Everybody standing at a Xerox machine is there to make copies. Yet in **1978**, **Ellen Langer, Arthur Blank, and Benzion Chanowitz** watched **93% of CUNY library users** smile and step aside (up from **60%** without that empty clause, SRC1) — AND then watched what happened when the stack grew to **20 pages**.

Recorded in 1978, CUNY Graduate Center Library, Manhattan, New York, the defining visual record centers on a sunlit 1978 university library copy alcove where a student holding 5 pages asks to cut at a beige Xerox photocopier beside a 93%-vs-24% compliance scoreboard (SRC1, SRC_STILL). In 1978 at CUNY's library photocopier, Ellen Langer proved that on small 5-page favors, an empty tautology ('because I have to make copies') boosted line-cutting yeses from 60% to 93% via scripted 'mindlessness' — yet on costly 20-page favors, only a genuine reason worked (42% vs 24%) (SRC1).

## 2. World before (era, place, why it mattered)

Classical attribution and decision theories assumed waking adults consciously read and weigh the logical content of every everyday request before saying yes or no.

At City University of New York (CUNY) Graduate Center Library, Manhattan in 1978, **Ellen Langer, Arthur Blank & Benzion Chanowitz's 1978 CUNY Graduate Center Library Xerox-Queue 'Mindlessness' Field Experiment** turned an unresolved question about human mind and brain function into a controlled, reproducible empirical test (SRC1: Ellen J. Langer, Arthur Blank, Benzion Chanowitz (1978). The mindlessness of ostensibly thoughtful action: The role of "placebic" information in interpersonal interaction. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology).

## 3. People

- **Ellen J. Langer, Arthur Blank & Benzion Chanowitz**: CUNY / Harvard social psychologists who tested whether busy adults process actual arguments or run on automatic linguistic scripts triggered by the single word 'because' (SRC1).
- **120 Unsuspecting CUNY Library Copy-Machine Users**: Busy students and scholars standing at the library Xerox machine who granted 93% line-cutting permission to a pure tautology when the cost was small (5 pages) — then woke right up when the cost hit 20 pages (SRC1).
- **Valerie S. Folkes & Robert B. Cialdini**: Consumer researcher who tested boundary conditions of controllability/refusal (1985) and persuasion theorist who immortalized Langer's Xerox queue as Chapter 1 ('Click, Whirr') of Influence (SRC2).

Ellen J. Langer (soon to become the first woman tenured in psychology at Harvard) teamed up with CUNY colleagues Arthur Blank and Benzion Chanowitz to test **scripted 'mindlessness'** right in the wild.

## 4. The event / experiment, step by step

In Study 3 at the CUNY Graduate Center library (SRC1), confederates approached **120 people** at the Xerox machine with either a **Small Favor (5 pages)** or a **Large Favor (20 pages)** using three scripts: **(1) Request Only**, **(2) Real Information** (*'...because I'm in a rush'*), or **(3) Placebic Information** (*'...because I have to make copies'*).

The sender adds one honest 'because' clause, and then teammate nods immediately and prioritizes it. At that moment, both teammates smooth, respectful collaboration. Never bark a bare deadline when one honest 'because' clause nearly doubles how gladly people help you (SRC1, SRC2).

## 5. Results (numbers as reported)

For **5 pages**, compliance leapt from **60% (No Reason)** to **94% (Real Reason)** and **93% (Placebic 'because I have to make copies'!)**. For **20 pages**, **Real Reason** still nearly doubled yeses (**42%** vs **24% No Reason**), whereas the hollow **Placebic 'because' crashed straight down to 24%** (SRC1).

- In **1978 (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*)**, **Ellen Langer, Arthur Blank, and Benzion Chanowitz** sent confederates up to **120 busy people** actively about to use the **Xerox photocopier** at the **CUNY Graduate Center library** with **three nearly identical phrasings**. (SRC1)
- When the favor was **SMALL (cutting in to copy 5 pages)**: Asking with **Request Only** (*'Excuse me, I have 5 pages. May I use the Xerox machine?'*) succeeded **60%** of the time. (SRC1)
- Adding a **Real Reason** (*'Excuse me, I have 5 pages. May I use the Xerox machine, **because I'm in a rush**?'*) catapulted compliance from **60% to 94%**. (SRC1)
- Then came the jaw-dropper — **Placebic (Empty) Information**: *'Excuse me, I have 5 pages. May I use the Xerox machine, **because I have to make copies**?'* Zero new information — every single human standing at a Xerox machine is there to make copies! — yet **93%** stepped aside and said *'Sure, go ahead!'*. (SRC1)
- Wait — **HALF THE INTERNET STOPS THERE AND MISSES THE SECOND HALF OF LANGER'S TABLE**. What happened in Condition 2 when the confederate asked to copy **20 PAGES** instead of 5 (a costly, annoying wait)? (SRC1)
- At **20 pages**, **Request Only** got **24%**, the **Real Reason** (*'because I'm in a rush'*) STILL doubled compliance to **42%** — and the fake tautology (*'because I have to make copies'*) **CRASHED ALL THE WAY BACK DOWN TO 24.0%** (statistically identical to giving no reason at all). (SRC1)
- Langer's 1978 paper actually opened with two office-memo studies too (**Studies 1 & 2, SRC1**): secretaries complied ~90% with interoffice memos formatted in familiar routine syntax regardless of empty wording, proving **'mindlessness'** isn't stupidity — it's an energy-saving **scripted autopilot** ('Request + Because + Something') that only runs when **stakes are low**. (SRC1)
- In 1985 (**Folkes, SRC2; Langer reply, SRC3**), boundary replications confirmed that whenever an ask is **expensive (20 pages), unusual, or controllable**, the brain snaps OUT of 'Click-Whirr' autopilot and audits every word after *'because'*. (SRC2)

Several quantitative contrasts stood out across the published data. Walk up to a library photocopier with 5 pages and say *'May I use the Xerox machine, **because I have to make copies**?'* — and **93% of New Yorkers step aside** (compared to 60% without that empty clause) — a striking result given that one syntactic connector word ('because') unlocked 93% compliance even when the clause after it meant literally nothing (SRC1). Try that **exact same meaningless 'because I have to make copies' line** holding **20 pages** instead of 5 — and compliance **plunges from 93% straight down to 24%**, while a real reason (*'because I'm in a rush'*) still hits **42%** — notable because pop-psych books call humans 'mindless zombies,' yet Langer's own 20-page column proved your brain switches its BS-detector ON the instant the price tag goes up (SRC1). Robert Cialdini named Langer's 5-page Xerox finding **'Click, Whirr'** — likening busy humans on low-stakes errands to mother turkeys triggered automatically by a single acoustic cue ('cheep-cheep' / 'because') — revealing how scripts save precious cognitive fuel on 5-page favors and yield to full logic on 20-page favors (SRC5).

## 6. Twist, controversy, misconceptions

Pop-business blogs love quoting ONLY the 93% number to claim *'because is a magic hypnosis word!'* — erasing the bottom half of Langer's own table. Langer's real discovery is far smarter: busy brains conserve energy on **cheap 20-second favors** by running a polite template (*'Request + Because + Something = Say Yes'*), yet the moment the **price rises to 20 pages** (or controllable inconvenience, Folkes 1985 SRC2; Langer 1985 SRC2), the brain snaps into **mindfulness** and rejects empty fluff.

- **"Because' is a magic hypnosis word that makes people obey huge, expensive requests for nonsense reasons."** — Langer's own 1978 Table 1 disproved this on the very same page. At **20 pages**, placebic *'because I have to make copies'* scored **24%** — 0% better than no reason at all (24%), whereas a genuine reason scored **42%**. (SRC1)
- **"The CUNY library students were careless or unintelligent."** — Graduate researchers at a Manhattan doctoral library fell for the 5-page script because **scripted processing is cognitively adaptive** when a favor only costs 20 seconds of standing. (SRC1)
- **"Giving people reasons in emails or UX prompts slows them down and lowers conversion."** — Even on real stakes (20 pages: 24% to 42%) and small stakes (5 pages: 60% to 94%), adding a **clear, honest 'because [real reason]'** nearly doubles voluntary cooperation. (SRC1)

## 7. What happened after (follow-ups, modern science)

Langer's Xerox study launched four decades of Mindlessness/Mindfulness science (SRC4), anchored Chapter 1 of Cialdini's *Influence*, and reshaped UX microcopy, leadership communication, and scam literacy.

Subsequent peer-reviewed studies tested, refined, and bounded these findings. **SRC2** (Valerie S. Folkes (1985). Mindlessness or mindfulness: A partial replication and extension of Langer, Blank, and Chanowitz. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) provided a controlled replication testing the 'placebic information' ('because I have to make copies') compliance heuristic across varying request sizes. **SRC3** (Ellen J Langer (1992). Matters of mind: Mindfulness/mindlessness in perspective. Consciousness and Cognition) presented Ellen Langer's theoretical synthesis explaining how familiar linguistic scripts trigger automatic mindless compliance. **SRC4** (Ellen J. Langer (1989). Minding Matters: The Consequences of Mindlessness–Mindfulness. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology) contributed Ellen Langer's Advances in Experimental Social Psychology review synthesizing scripted automaticity and mindful attention. **SRC5** (Robert B. Cialdini, Noah J. Goldstein (2004). Social Influence: Compliance and Conformity. Annual Review of Psychology) offered a comprehensive review situating Langer's automatic 'click, whirr' compliance trigger within social influence science.

## 8. What it means for you

In 1978 at CUNY's library photocopier, Ellen Langer proved that on small 5-page favors, an empty tautology ('because I have to make copies') boosted line-cutting yeses from 60% to 93% via scripted 'mindlessness' — yet on costly 20-page favors, only a genuine reason worked (42% vs 24%). Two rules for Monday morning: (1) Always attach a real, specific **'because...'** when asking busy colleagues for help; (2) Whenever someone asks YOU for time or money, mentally cover the word 'because' and check whether the second half actually says anything.

Pair every favor or deadline you ask for with a **genuine, one-clause 'because'**, and whenever a pitch asks YOU for a 20-page commitment, read the words *after* 'because' out loud.

Core scientific concepts from this research translate into everyday terms as follows:

- **placebic information**: a grammatically complete 'reason' clause ('because I have to make copies') that adds zero new facts yet satisfies a polite social script — comparable to a sugar pill made out of grammar.
- **mindlessness (scripted processing)**: relying on overlearned social templates ('Polite Ask + Because = Say Yes') during low-cost moments instead of parsing every word — comparable to skimming a green light on a familiar street.
- **5-page vs 20-page stake switch**: Langer's built-in proof that rising effort cost (20 pages) shuts off mindless autopilot (93% to 24%) and turns on genuine argument scrutiny (42%) — comparable to waking up the security guard when the bill exceeds $5.
- **mindfulness (Langerian)**: actively noticing novel distinctions in the present context rather than running yesterday's script — comparable to reading the actual words after 'because'.

In daily life, this mechanism surfaces in familiar situations:

- Writing a quick Slack request or calendar change WITH a crisp honest *'because [specific deadline]'* instead of a bare demand — and getting an instant 'Yes!'.
- Catching yourself nodding along to a smooth-sounding sales pitch ('You need the Pro tier because you want great results!') and pausing to ask: *'Wait — what came AFTER the word because?'*.
- Explaining rules to kids or teammates with a real *'because'* rationale instead of *'just do it'*.
